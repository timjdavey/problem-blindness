# Equal vs unequal error costs

An axis in the [Stakes](index.md#stakes) set of the [performance](index.md#performance) group.

Do a false alarm and a miss cost about the same, or is one mistake far worse than the other? A smoke alarm that goes off over toast costs you a minute. One that stays quiet in a fire can cost your life, so it should be set to cry wolf. A court leans the other way and would rather free the guilty than jail the innocent. The cost of a miss can also be a prize you never get. An investor who passes on the next Google loses more than one who backs ten duds. Where to set the bar for saying yes depends on how likely each case is, on what each mistake costs and on what each right call wins.

## Actions

**Equal error costs**: Maximise accuracy.
- Set the bar at even odds. A false alarm and a miss cost the same, so take the umbrella when rain is more likely than not. [1]
- Keep one score: how often you're right. It tells you all you need when both errors weigh the same. [1]

**Unequal error costs**: Set the threshold from the costs and the gains.
- Put a price on each kind of error, in the same units, and count what a hit wins as well as what a miss loses. Blackstone judged it better that ten guilty people go free than that one innocent suffers. [1]
- When a hit pays far more than a false alarm costs, set the bar low and take many shots. Haselton and Buss argued that men overrate a woman's interest because, over evolution, a missed chance cost more than a rebuff. [1]
- Set the bar from those prices and from how common the real thing is. A cancer screen should accept many false alarms to avoid missing a cancer. How common it is has its own axis, [High vs low base rate](high-vs-low-base-rate.md). [1]
- Check that a lower bar still catches more. A 2003 study found American breast screening called back far more women than British screening, yet found about the same number of cancers. [1]
- Count the two kinds of mistake separately, never accuracy alone. One score that adds them up treats a missed cancer and a false scare as the same. [1]
- Plan for the false alarms. Crying wolf pays only while each alarm is cheap to check, as with each bag the airport scanner flags. [1]

## Correlated with

- [High vs low base rate](high-vs-low-base-rate.md) (tension). These are the two things signal detection theory sets the bar from. A rare target says raise the bar, since most yeses will be false. A costly miss says lower it. Cancer screening sits at both hard ends.
- [Survivable vs ruinous](survivable-vs-ruinous.md) (logic). When one kind of mistake can ruin you and the other can't, the costs are as lopsided as they get.
- [Direct vs proxy measure](direct-vs-proxy-measure.md) (logic). A score that counts every mistake the same leaves the costs out. A model tuned for plain accuracy treats a missed cancer and a false scare as equal.
- [Shared vs contested values](shared-vs-contested-values.md) (logic). How much worse a miss is than a false alarm is a value judgement, so people fight over where to set the bar.
- [Thin vs fat tails](thin-vs-fat-tails.md) (logic). When one rare hit can pay more than all the duds put together, missing it is the costly mistake, so the bar for saying yes drops. The two are different. Fat tails is about how outcomes spread. This axis is about where to set the bar on one yes or no call.
