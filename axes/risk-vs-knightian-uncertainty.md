# Risk vs Knightian uncertainty

An axis in the [Uncertainty](index.md#uncertainty) set of the [information](index.md#information) group.

Can you put a number you trust on how likely each outcome is, or do you only know that things are uncertain? A dice roll has known odds. Whether a new technology takes off does not, and pretending it does gives you false confidence. In 1921 the economist Frank Knight called doubt you can put a number on risk, and doubt you can't put a number on uncertainty. Since "uncertainty" now gets used for both, the second kind is called Knightian uncertainty.

## Actions

**Measurable risk**: Compute and optimise.
- Use expected value as the test. With odds you trust, the best choice is the one that does best on average. [1]
- Let a calibrated forecast make the call. If a forecaster's seven in ten chance of rain comes true on seven days in ten, trust it over a hunch. [1, 4]

**Knightian uncertainty**: Don't trust a forecast's odds just because they are precise.
- Test plans against several plausible futures, not one forecast. A choice that is fine in all of them beats one that is best in one and bad in the rest. [5, 6, 7, 8]
- Set signs to watch, and be ready to switch. London's Thames Estuary 2100 plan, published in 2012, moves to a bigger flood defence sooner if the sea rises faster than expected. [5, 6, 7]
- Keep a margin of safety. Assume your estimate of the odds is wrong and leave room for it. A founder keeps more cash than the plan says they need. [1]
- Make small bets you can grow. In the early days of the web, firms that kept many small stakes could grow the ones that worked, while most that bet on one guess lost. [3]
- Prefer simple rules to finely tuned models. In a 2009 test none of 14 tuned investment models reliably beat splitting the money equally. [4]
- Don't treat unknown odds as bad odds. Shunning an option only because its odds are a blank means paying to stick with the familiar, as savers do when they keep most of their money in home shares. [2]
- Ask what is going on before you ask for odds. Past calibration says nothing about a case with no past, so build the best story that fits the facts and test it against what others think. [1, 8]

## Correlated with

- [Plentiful vs scarce data](plentiful-vs-scarce-data.md) (logic). [Knight](../frameworks/1921-risk-vs-uncertainty.md)'s known odds come from a long record of similar cases.
- [Stationary vs drifting](stationary-vs-drifting.md) (logic). Knight's uncertainty starts where the future may not look like the past.
- [Thin vs fat tails](thin-vs-fat-tails.md) (logic). Tail odds can't be read off a calm past ([Mediocristan vs Extremistan](../frameworks/2007-mediocristan-vs-extremistan.md)).
- [Shared vs contested values](shared-vs-contested-values.md) (bundled). [Deep uncertainty](../frameworks/2003-deep-uncertainty.md) and [Post-normal science](../frameworks/1993-post-normal-science.md) put unknown odds and disputed values in one box. [Stirling](../frameworks/2007-incertitude-matrix.md) and [Thompson and Tuden](../frameworks/1959-decision-strategies-matrix.md) keep them apart, so they go together in practice but not by definition.
- [Reducible vs irreducible uncertainty](reducible-vs-irreducible-uncertainty.md) (independent). These two get mixed up more than any other pair. Risk vs Knightian uncertainty asks whether you can put a number on your doubt. Reducible asks whether more data would shrink it. A coin toss has known odds and doubt you can't shrink. A new drug has unknown odds and doubt you can shrink, because you can run a trial. Doubt that comes from pure chance is called aleatory, and doubt that comes from missing knowledge is called epistemic.
- [Small vs large world](small-vs-large-world.md) (by definition). You can't put odds on a state you never listed. But Ellsberg's urn has only two states and the odds are still unknown ([Ambiguity aversion](../frameworks/1961-ambiguity-aversion.md)).
