# Satisficing vs optimising

An axis in the [Goal](index.md#goal) set of the [performance](index.md#performance) group.

Will any answer that clears the bar do, or does each extra bit of quality still pay off, so you need the best you can find? For a lunch spot, any decent place will do. Setting the dose of a strong drug, or tuning a racing car, is worth every extra bit of care.

## Actions

**Satisficing**: Set a bar and stop when you clear it.
- Take the first option that meets the need. Searching for the very best costs more than it is worth in most settings, so pick the first decent lunch spot and eat. [1, 2]
- Write the bar down before you start, and lower it if nothing clears it for a long time. Without a bar, a house hunt or a redesign drags on because better always seems possible. [1]
- Don't polish the weights. Near the best answer the payoff is often flat, so any sensible weights do about as well. When Robyn Dawes predicted how faculty would rate graduate students, adding three scores with equal weights did better than fitted weights. [2]

**Optimising**: Spend on the last few percent, and check you got it.
- Search wider and tune with care. Where the payoff has a sharp peak, a small miss costs a lot. Patients on the blood thinner warfarin have regular blood tests because the safe dose window is narrow. [2]
- Confirm the gain on cases you didn't tune on. Tuning fits noise as well as signal. In a contest across 20 data sets, regression came first on data it had already seen, then lost the most on new data. [3]
- Price the last step before you pay for it. Ask what the gap between good and best is worth and what it costs to close. Many payoffs are flat near the top, and there a long hunt wastes effort. [1, 2]

## Correlated with

- [Forgiving vs exacting](forgiving-vs-exacting.md) and [Tractable vs intractable options](tractable-vs-intractable-options.md) (tension). Needing the best answer when the land is rugged or the search is too big is the hardest case. Small steps stop on the nearest peak, and the [NP problems](../frameworks/1971-np-problems.md) note advises settling for a good answer.
- [Ample time vs time pressure](ample-time-vs-time-pressure.md) (tension). Needing the best answer with no time to look for it is a clash. Simon's point was that limited time and attention push people to good enough, and Klein's fire commanders took the first plan that would work rather than compare options ([Satisficing](../frameworks/1956-satisficing.md)).
- [Single objective vs trade-offs](single-objective-vs-trade-offs.md) (opposite). Simon's own model of satisficing works this way. In 1955 he proposed that people with several goals set a floor on each and take the first option that clears them all ([A behavioral model of rational choice](https://doi.org/10.2307/1884852)).
- [Verifiable vs unverifiable](verifiable-vs-unverifiable.md) (tension). You can't claim you found the best answer if you can't check it.
- [Convergent vs divergent](convergent-vs-divergent.md) (independent). Many good answers don't settle whether extra quality pays. A wedding speech only needs to be good. An advert has just as many good answers, yet each extra bit of quality still pays off in sales.
- [Curable vs chronic](curable-vs-chronic.md) (independent). Optimising can feel endless, but a drug dose gets tuned once and stays tuned. A lawn only needs to look fine, yet it needs mowing every week.
- [Bounded vs open-ended skill](bounded-vs-open-ended-skill.md) (independent). This axis asks whether the payoff has a ceiling. That one asks whether the skill does. A thank you note only needs to be warm, though writing well has no ceiling.
