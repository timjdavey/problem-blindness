# Winnable vs damage limitation

An axis in the [Stakes](index.md#stakes) set of the [decision](index.md#the-decision) group.

Can you come out ahead, or is the best outcome on offer still a loss? In 2002 and 2003 Grigori Perelman posted a proof of the Poincaré conjecture, one of the Millennium Prize problems. It was done, with nothing left to regret. On 15 January 2009 US Airways Flight 1549 hit a flock of geese about two minutes after leaving LaGuardia and lost both engines. Chesley Sullenberger and Jeffrey Skiles put it down on the Hudson, and all 155 people on board survived. That is about as good as the outcome could have been, and the plane was still lost.

Even then, the inquiry ran simulations to check whether the plane could have made it back to an airport. Pilots who turned back the moment the birds hit reached a runway in 8 of 15 runs. The one run that first allowed 35 seconds to take in what had happened, as a real crew would need, didn't make it. A damage limitation problem keeps getting judged against a perfect outcome that was never really on offer.

This axis asks how good the best case is, not how bad the worst case is. A falling market shows the difference. You can trade out and back in, spread your money and wait, and no single move will ruin you. But every option loses money, and the best you can do is lose less. Work where success is invisible has the same shape. Nobody notices the planes that land safely or the servers that stay up, so the best you can earn is no complaints.

Two things go wrong at this end. First, people facing a sure loss will gamble to escape it, even at worse odds. Kahneman and Tversky found this in 1979 and built it into [Prospect theory](../frameworks/1979-prospect-theory.md). Second, people judge a decision by how it turned out, so whoever made a good call in a bad spot gets blamed for the loss. Baron and Hershey called this [Outcome bias](../frameworks/1988-outcome-bias.md).

## Actions

**Winnable**: Aim for the win and score it plainly.
- Score the model against the ideal answer. A clean win is on offer, so falling short of it is a real miss.
- Judge by the result. When a clean win is possible, the result tells you a lot about the decision.

**Damage limitation**: Set the baseline before you act, and score against it.
- Write down what doing nothing would cost and what the best realistic outcome is, before you act. Score the model on how much loss it avoided, not against a perfect result.
- Cap losses in advance, and give the call to stop to someone who isn't carrying the loss. Nick Leeson kept raising his bets to win back what he had lost, and broke Barings Bank in 1995 ([Prospect theory](../frameworks/1979-prospect-theory.md)).
- Keep a record of what was known and why each choice was made. Judge the decision against that, not against how it turned out ([Outcome bias](../frameworks/1988-outcome-bias.md)).
- Check the model commits to the least bad option. A model rewarded for good outcomes can hedge, or keep searching for a clean win that isn't there.
- Expect blame whatever happens, and support the people who decide. They will hold themselves to the perfect result too.

## Correlated with

- [Slack vs time pressure](slack-vs-time-pressure.md) (study). Crises are often no-win, which is part of why Grint's critical problems, like a fire in a tower block, call for command ([Tame, wicked and critical](../frameworks/2005-tame-wicked-and-critical.md)). But a falling market gives you time to think and still only offers losses.
- [Survivable vs ruinous](survivable-vs-ruinous.md) (independent). That axis is about how bad the worst case is, and this one is about how good the best case is. A tightrope walk has ruin as its worst case and a clean win as its best. Choosing which of two friends to let down is no-win but small.
- [Private vs accountable](private-vs-accountable.md) (logic). Anyone who must defend a no-win choice will be judged by the loss. A record of what was known at the time is the main defence against [outcome bias](../frameworks/1988-outcome-bias.md).
- [Shared vs contested values](shared-vs-contested-values.md) (logic). When a loss has to land somewhere, people fight over where. Every line in a budget cut has someone who loses. But everyone on Flight 1549 wanted the same thing.
- [Single vs multiple objectives](single-vs-multiple-objectives.md) (bundled). Tetlock's tragic trade-offs, such as choosing which of two sick boys to treat, set one sacred value against another, so some loss is certain ([Taboo trade-offs](../frameworks/1997-taboo-trade-offs.md)). But Sullenberger had one objective and still couldn't win.
- [Complete vs incomplete score](complete-vs-incomplete-score.md) (logic). A score measured from zero makes every damage limitation outcome look like failure. What would have happened anyway has to be part of the score.
- [Ends vs never-ending](ends-vs-never-ending.md) (independent). Carse's finite game both ends and can be won, but the two traits come apart. A crash landing is over in minutes and can't be won. A farm never finishes, yet it can have a good harvest every year.
