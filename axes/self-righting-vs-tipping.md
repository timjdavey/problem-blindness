# Self-righting vs tipping

An axis in the [Stimulus](index.md#stimulus) set of the [environment](index.md#environment) group.

When something pushes the system, does it go back to where it was or stay where it was pushed? Or does it flip into a new state and stay there? Picture a ball. In a bowl it rolls back to the bottom. On a flat table it stays wherever you leave it. On a hilltop a small nudge sends it off the edge, and it won't roll back up. Materials show the same three cases. Elastic things spring back, plastic things stay bent and brittle things snap.

This is a question about the system, not about you. [Reversible vs irreversible](reversible-vs-irreversible.md) asks whether you can undo your own move. Deleting a paragraph in Word is reversible, because you can press undo. But the document never puts the paragraph back by itself, so it sits in the middle of this axis. We call that middle malleable. It takes whatever shape you push it into and keeps it.

There are two ways to be self-righting, and Taleb lumps them together ([Fragile, robust and antifragile](../frameworks/2012-fragile-robust-and-antifragile.md)). A robust system barely moves when pushed, like your body holding its temperature on a hot day. A resilient system moves a long way and still comes back, like a forest growing back after a fire. In 1973 the ecologist C. S. Holling split these two apart ([Resilience and stability](../frameworks/1973-resilience-and-stability.md)). He said speed of return was one thing, and the size of push a system can take before it flips was another.

At the far end sit systems with a tipping point. A shallow lake fed more and more fertiliser from farms stays clear for years, then turns murky in a season. Cutting the fertiliser back to where it was doesn't bring the clear water back. You have to cut it much further. This is called hysteresis, and it means stopping the push is not the same as undoing it ([Critical transitions](../frameworks/2001-critical-transitions.md)).

Neither end is good or bad in itself. It depends on which state you want. A system that rights itself after your mistakes also rights itself after your fixes. And the stickiness that keeps a lake murky will keep it clear once you get it back. When the state on the far side is the one you want, a tipping point is the way in.

## Actions

**Self-righting**: Let small errors wash out.
- Delegate freely, whether to a junior or a machine. The system absorbs a bad move and returns to normal, so one mistake costs little. [1, 4]
- Measure your errors directly, not by watching the system. A system that absorbs your mistakes also hides them, so you can be wrong for a long time without seeing it. [1]
- Don't damp every swing. Holding a system steady can wear away its resilience without anyone noticing. [1]
- Expect your fixes to wash out as well as your mistakes. Find what pulls the system back, and change that rather than pushing harder. Six years after The Biggest Loser, 14 contestants had regained on average 41 of the 58 kg they lost, and their bodies still burned about 500 calories a day less than expected for their size. [6]

**Malleable**: Check the state after each action.
- Check what the system holds after each step, not what you told it to do. At Three Mile Island a light showed that a valve had been told to close, not that it had closed, and coolant drained for over two hours. [2]
- Keep an undo trail. The system won't recover, so recovery has to come from you. Coca-Cola could bring back its old drink in 1985 because it had kept the recipe. [5]

**Tipping point**: Find the edge, then stay well clear of it or push right past it.
- Find the thresholds before you push. A system near its edge can look just as it always has, so ask what level of load, traffic or nudging has flipped this kind of system before. [1, 3]
- Keep a wide margin. The flip is fast and the way back is long, so the cost of getting close is far bigger than it looks. [3]
- Watch how fast the system recovers from small pushes. Recovery slows as a system nears its tipping point, and that slowing is one of the few warnings you get. [3]
- Don't plan on reversing the input. Trust that a manager has lost won't come back the day the bad policy goes, and a habit an app has nudged people into won't end when the nudge stops. [3]
- Budget for a far bigger push to get back, often of a new kind. Some lakes in the Netherlands and Denmark only cleared once managers also removed the fish that stir up the mud. [3]
- Judge many small pushes together, not one at a time. Each extra demand on a stretched team looks small on its own. The flip comes from the sum. [1, 3]
- When the far side is better, push hard enough to cross in one go. A push that stops short slides back and looks like failure. Once over, the new state holds itself, as the clear Dutch and Danish lakes did once their plants returned. In a 2018 experiment, groups of 20 kept their old naming habit when 4 members pushed a new one, and switched almost completely when 5 did. [3, 7]

## Correlated with

- [Stable vs chaotic](stable-vs-chaotic.md) (independent). Both are about small pushes growing, but in different ways. Chaos means small differences grow all the time, while the system stays the same kind of system. A tipping point means a system that holds steady until one threshold, then jumps. Day to day weather is chaotic, yet global temperature came back within a few years of the 1991 Pinatubo eruption cooling it by about half a degree. A shallow lake is easy to forecast on either side of its threshold and still tips.
- [Reversible vs irreversible](reversible-vs-irreversible.md) (independent). That axis is about your undo button and this one is about the system. A Word document is reversible and malleable. A felled forest can't be un-felled, yet it grows back. A lake whose fertiliser you can stop at any time still stays murky.
- [Coherent vs incoherent runs](coherent-vs-incoherent-runs.md) (logic). A system with two stable states sends runs that start close together to different places, so the average of the runs describes neither.
- [Clustered vs scattered runs](clustered-vs-scattered-runs.md) (logic). A tipping system has a few stable states, so its runs fall into a few clear groups.
- [Buffered vs cascading](buffered-vs-cascading.md) (logic). A cascade is a tip that spreads from one part to the next.
- [Durable vs perishable lessons](durable-vs-perishable-lessons.md) (independent). In drift the rules slowly change. In a tip the rules stay fixed and the state jumps. Slang drifts with no tipping point, and the lake tips while the chemistry stays the same.
- [Survivable vs ruinous](survivable-vs-ruinous.md) (logic). A tip you can't climb back from is ruin for whoever depends on the old state.
- [Antifragile vs wears out](antifragile-vs-wears-out.md) (independent). This axis asks where the system settles after one push. That one asks how the system itself has changed after many. A bridge rights itself after every lorry and still wears out.
- [Flat vs compounding results](flat-vs-compounding-results.md) (logic). When each adopter makes a technology more attractive to the next, the market tips to one of them and stays there, as Arthur's model shows ([Increasing returns](../frameworks/1989-increasing-returns.md)). This axis is about the state of the system, and that one is about your own base.
