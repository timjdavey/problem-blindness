# Flat vs compounding results

An axis in the [Stakes](index.md#stakes) set of the [performance](index.md#performance) group.

Does each result leave you where you started, or does it change what you bring to the next round? A pieceworker paid per item starts each day afresh, so a bad day costs that day's pay and nothing more. Savings compound, and so do a reputation, an audience, a skill, a team's trust and a pile of technical debt. Each gain grows the base the next round builds on, and each loss shrinks it. So a mistake costs more than the round it happened in, and one path through time can end far from the average.

Money shows it most cleanly. In Ole Peters' coin game heads grows your wealth by 50% and tails shrinks it by 40%. The crowd's average rises 5% a round, while a typical player loses about 5% a round. When the crowd's average and one player's long run result differ like this, the game is called non-ergodic ([Ergodicity](../frameworks/2016-ergodicity.md)).

Credit works the same way. Robert Merton noticed that famous scientists get more credit than unknown ones for the same work, so early recognition snowballs. He called it the Matthew effect ([Matthew effect](../frameworks/1968-matthew-effect.md)). In the Netherlands, researchers who scored just above the cut-off for a grant went on to win more than twice as much funding over the next eight years as those just below, though reviewers had rated the two groups almost the same.

## Actions

**Flat results**: Judge by the average per round.
- Use expected value per decision as the test. Gains and losses sum, so the average is what you get. [3]
- Don't pay to insure a loss you can easily absorb. The insurer only signs if it expects to profit, so on average the buyer loses. [3]

**Compounding results**: Judge by what one path does over time.
- Play out a long run of results as they compound, not a single step. A trader who wins on average per trade can still drain the account, since a 50% gain after a 40% loss still leaves you 10% down. [3]
- Report the typical path, not the average one. In the coin game over 100 rounds the average ends near £13,150 from a £100 start, while the most likely result is about 52p. [3]
- Commit a share of what you have each round, never the lot. The Kelly rule sets each stake this way, so a run of losses can't sink you. [3]
- Pay to insure against a loss that would cut deep, even at a price above its average cost. Peters and Adamou argued that a family a house fire would ruin grows richer over time by paying the premium. [3]
- Put effort in early, when a small lead is cheapest to win and grows the most. In Brian Arthur's model a few chance choices by early adopters decide which technology takes the whole market. [2]
- Don't read a leader's lead as proof it was better. In an online music market the same songs ended at very different ranks in separate worlds once listeners could see what others downloaded. The best songs rarely did badly and the worst rarely did well, but anything else could happen. [1]
- Keep competing after a near miss. Part of the gap between Dutch researchers just above and just below the grant cut-off came from those below it no longer applying for other funding. [1]

## Correlated with

- [Survivable vs ruinous](survivable-vs-ruinous.md) (by definition). Ruin is the extreme case, since a ruined player gets nothing over time whatever the crowd's average says. But the gap can open without ruin, and the coin game never wipes anyone out.
- [Thin vs fat tails](thin-vs-fat-tails.md) (logic). Compounding piles the gains on a lucky few. In the coin game the crowd's average keeps rising only because a handful of players get vastly rich, and the music market made its hits bigger once listeners could see each other's choices.
- [Standalone vs sequential](standalone-vs-sequential.md) (by definition). That axis asks whether a choice shapes the next choice, and this one asks whether a result changes your base. Compounding makes the rounds depend on each other, since each result sets the stake for the next. But a chess game is sequential with no base to compound, and betting half your savings every round repeats the same choice yet compounds.
- [Coherent vs incoherent runs](coherent-vs-incoherent-runs.md) (logic). Compounding splits players' paths apart, so the crowd's average describes almost nobody. That is incoherence across players over time.
- [Self-righting vs tipping](self-righting-vs-tipping.md) (logic). When each adopter makes a technology more attractive to the next, the market tips to one of them and stays there, as Arthur's model shows ([Increasing returns](../frameworks/1989-increasing-returns.md)). Tipping is about the state of the system, and this axis is about your own base.
- [Antifragile vs wears out](antifragile-vs-wears-out.md) (independent). That axis asks what stress does to the system, and this one asks what your own results do to what you have next time. Savings compound with no stress at all, and a tyre wears out with no result to carry forward.
