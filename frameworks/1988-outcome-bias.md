# Outcome bias

**People judge a decision by how it turned out, not by what the person knew when they made it.**

1988 · Jonathan Baron and John Hershey · [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.54.4.569) · Feeds 4 axes

## The idea

In 1988 Jonathan Baron and John Hershey published "Outcome bias in decision evaluation" in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. They gave students cases in which someone made a choice under risk, such as a surgeon deciding whether to operate on a patient with a heart condition. Every student got the same facts and the same odds of success. The only thing that changed was whether the patient lived or died. Students were asked to rate the thinking behind the decision, yet they rated it worse when the patient died. They also rated the surgeon as less competent.

This is different from hindsight bias, where you think you knew all along how things would turn out. Outcome bias can happen even when you admit nobody could have known. The result leaks into your judgement of the choice anyway.

The trait this sorts on is whether a decision gets judged by how it was made or by how it turned out. Over many decisions the two line up, since good choices win more often. On a single decision luck can swamp skill.

## What goes wrong

The mistake is to grade people, and models, on outcomes alone. That punishes good calls in a bad spot and rewards reckless calls that got lucky. Annie Duke, a professional poker player, calls the mistake "resulting" in *Thinking in Bets* (2018).

## What to do instead

The better response is to write down what was known and why each choice was made at the time, and to judge the choice against that record.

## Examples

### The worst call in Super Bowl history

On 1 February 2015 the Seattle Seahawks were losing 28 to 24 to the New England Patriots with 26 seconds left. They had the ball on the one yard line, and it was second down. Everyone expected them to give it to Marshawn Lynch to run. Pete Carroll, the coach, called a pass instead, and Malcolm Butler intercepted it. Within minutes it was being called the worst call in Super Bowl history. Several analysts later argued it was sound. With one timeout left, a pass that fell incomplete would stop the clock, which gave Seattle time for three tries at the end zone instead of two. If the pass had been caught, few would have questioned it.

### The surgeon's record

A surgeon who takes on the hardest cases will lose more patients than one who turns them away. Judged on death rates alone, the careful surgeon looks worse. In 1990 New York State began publishing death rates for heart bypass surgery, and it later named each surgeon. In a survey of the state's heart surgeons published in 1999, 62% of those who replied said they had refused at least one high-risk patient in the past year, mainly because of the reports. Judging by outcome taught them to avoid the patients who most needed a good surgeon.
