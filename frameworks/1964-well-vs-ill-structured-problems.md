# Well vs ill-structured problems

Some problems come with everything you need to know whether you have solved them. The goal is clear, the moves you can make are known and there is a test that tells a good answer from a bad one. Chess is like this. Other problems are missing one or more of those parts. You don't fully know what you want, what you are allowed to do or how to tell when you are done. Herbert Simon and his colleague Allen Newell called the two kinds well-structured and ill-structured as early as 1958. In 1964 the psychologist Walter Reitman called the second kind ill-defined problems. In 1973 Simon gave his full account of the split.

Simon's point was that the tidy kind is rare outside games and textbooks. Designing a house is his classic ill-structured problem. The client can't list every want up front, and the wants change once they see a sketch. Each choice about the roof changes what makes sense for the windows. Simon thought people cope by carving off small well-structured pieces as they go, and pinning down the goal a little more each time.

The usual mistake is to treat an ill-structured problem as if it were well-structured. You write a full specification and fix the budget and the date. Then you act surprised when the real goal turns up halfway through. The reverse mistake also happens. People treat a problem with a clear answer as if it were open to endless debate, and so never sit down and work it out.

## Examples

**Deep Blue.** In May 1997 IBM's Deep Blue beat the world champion Garry Kasparov over six games in New York. Chess is hard but well-structured. The board, the legal moves and the meaning of a win are all fixed, so a machine could search its way to strength. That same approach is useless where the moves and the goal are themselves unclear.

**Sydney Opera House.** In 1957 Jørn Utzon won the design competition with a sketch of curved shells that nobody yet knew how to build. The project was run as if it had a fixed plan, with a budget of about A$7 million and an opening planned for 1963. Working out how to build the roof took years. Utzon quit in 1966, and the building finally opened in 1973 at a cost of A$102 million. The problem was ill-structured from the start, and the budget pretended it wasn't.

**A crossword and a holiday.** A crossword has one right grid, and you know the moment you have it. Planning a family holiday has no such test. You only learn what people want as options appear, and someone always changes their mind once the flights are booked.
