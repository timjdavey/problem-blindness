# Programmed vs non-programmed decisions

In 1960 Herbert Simon published a short book called *The New Science of Management Decision*. Simon studied how firms really make choices, and he was also writing some of the first programs that could solve problems. The book asked which decisions could be handed to a procedure.

He sorted decisions by how often they come round in the same form. Some repeat again and again, like reordering stock when a shelf runs low. A firm can work out a procedure once and follow it. Simon called these programmed decisions, taking the word from computing. Others are new, have no clear shape and carry high stakes, like whether to enter a new market. No procedure exists, so people fall back on judgement and rules of thumb. These are non-programmed decisions. Simon saw this as a scale, not two boxes. In 1971 Anthony Gorry and Michael Scott Morton at MIT renamed the ends structured and unstructured, with semi-structured in between. Their grid became the basis of decision support systems.

The mistake runs both ways. Write a procedure for a decision that isn't routine and it will be confidently wrong on every case it didn't foresee. Treat a routine decision as special each time and you waste judgement and get different answers to the same question. Simon and James March had also warned in 1958 that daily routine drives out planning. The better response is to ask two questions. How often does this decision come round in the same form, and how costly is a wrong call? Programme what truly repeats and keep people on what doesn't.

## Examples

**Robodebt.** From 2016 to 2019 Australia's welfare agency checked benefit claims against tax records by computer. It took a person's yearly income and spread it evenly across the year's 26 fortnights. If that average was higher than what they had reported for a fortnight, the system raised a debt. Staff checks were largely removed. But many people on benefits work irregular hours, so the average was wrong for exactly the people being checked. About 400,000 people were issued unlawful debts. In 2021 a court approved a settlement worth A$1.8 billion, and in 2023 a royal commission called the scheme "crude and cruel".

**The surgical checklist.** Surgery feels like the opposite of routine. Yet some steps repeat in every operation, like confirming the patient and the site, checking for allergies and counting swabs. In 2007 and 2008 a team led by Alex Haynes and Atul Gawande tested a 19-item checklist from the World Health Organization in eight hospitals around the world. Deaths fell from 1.5% to 0.8%, and complications fell from 11% to 7%. Programming the routine steps freed the surgeons' judgement for the rest.
