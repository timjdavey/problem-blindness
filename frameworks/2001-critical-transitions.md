# Critical transitions

**Many systems have more than one stable state, and a slow, steady push can flip one into the other in a season.**

2001 (2009) · Marten Scheffer, Stephen Carpenter, Jonathan Foley, Carl Folke and Brian Walker · [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.1038/35098000) · Feeds 1 axis

## The idea

A shallow lake in its natural state is clear, with beds of water plants on the bottom. The plants hold the mud down and soak up nutrients, which keeps the algae in check and the water clear. Now let fertiliser wash in from the farms around it, a little more each year. For a long time nothing seems to happen. Then one summer the algae bloom, the water turns green, the plants die in the shade and the mud gets stirred up. The lake is now murky, and the murk keeps itself going just as the clear water did.

Marten Scheffer, a Dutch ecologist, and four colleagues set out the general pattern in Nature in 2001. Many systems have two or more stable states, each one holding itself in place. A slow push makes the current state weaker without changing how it looks, until a small extra push tips it over. We call this a critical transition, or a tipping point. They found the same shape in lakes, coral reefs, forests, deserts and oceans.

## What goes wrong

The worst part is that the way back is not the way in. Cutting the fertiliser to where it was when the lake turned doesn't clear it. You have to cut far below that level, and often remove fish or replant by hand as well. This is called hysteresis.

In 2009 Scheffer and another group of colleagues asked whether you could see a tip coming. Their answer was a qualified yes. As a system nears the edge it gets slower to recover from small knocks, so it wanders further and stays off course for longer. This is called critical slowing down. It shows up as wider swings and as each day looking more like the last.

## Examples

### Peter Lake

In 2011 Stephen Carpenter's team tested the warning signs on a whole lake in northern Wisconsin. Over three years they added largemouth bass to Peter Lake, a few at a time, to push its food web towards a new state. Paul Lake, next door, was left alone for comparison. In the third year the plankton in Peter Lake swung more widely and recovered more slowly, more than a year before the food web finished changing. Paul Lake showed nothing of the kind.

### Restoring a lake

Lake managers in the Netherlands and Denmark found that cutting nutrients alone often left lakes murky for years. Some only cleared after the managers also removed most of the fish that stir up the mud, which gave the plants a chance to come back. Once the plants returned, the clear state held itself in place again.
