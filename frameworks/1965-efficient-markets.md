# Efficient markets

**Where many people trade on the same facts, the price already carries them. An edge anyone can see has already been taken.**

1965 (1970) · Eugene Fama · [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.1086/294743) · Feeds 1 axis

## The idea

In 1900 Louis Bachelier argued in his Paris thesis that stock prices move at random, because every expectation people hold is already in the price. Nobody took much notice for fifty years. In 1965 Paul Samuelson proved that properly anticipated prices must fluctuate at random, and Eugene Fama's Chicago thesis of the same year found that stock prices did. In 1970 Fama pulled the theory and the evidence together in the *Journal of Finance* and named three forms. In a weak form market, past prices tell you nothing about future ones. In a semi-strong one, nothing public does. In a strong one, nothing private does either.

The idea rests on competition. If a stock is plainly cheap, whoever sees it first buys until it isn't. So any edge that can be found by anyone with the same facts is gone by the time you act on it. The trait this sorts on is how many people are already acting on the facts you have. Where many are, the answer is in the price before you get there.

In 1980 Sanford Grossman and Joseph Stiglitz showed the idea can't be quite true. If prices carried every fact, nobody would be paid to gather facts, so nobody would, and the prices would stop carrying them. A market can only be as efficient as the cost of finding things out allows. What is left over pays for information that costs something to get. That is the answer to the old joke about the economist who won't pick up a note on the pavement because if it were real someone would have taken it. Someone will take it, and it will be whoever walked that street when nobody else had.

## What goes wrong

The mistake is to treat a visible edge as free money. A pattern anyone could have spotted in the price history has been spotted. A tip that reached you has reached others. The same holds outside markets. A business idea that needs no special knowledge and no special access has been tried, and if it isn't everywhere, there is usually a reason. Burton Malkiel's 2003 review found markets more efficient, and less predictable, than the papers claiming to beat them.

## What to do instead

The better response is to ask why this is still on offer. If the answer is that nobody else has the facts, the access or the patience, the edge may be real. If the answer is that nobody else has noticed, assume they have, and that the price says so. Where you can't name the cost you paid to know something others don't, expect to earn what the market earns, and pay as little as you can for the privilege.

## Examples

### The ten year bet

In 2008 Warren Buffett bet a firm called Protégé Partners that a plain index fund tracking the S&P 500 would beat any five funds of hedge funds they cared to pick over ten years. Protégé picked five, which between them held stakes in more than 200 hedge funds. Over 2008 to 2017 the index fund gained 125.8%. The five funds of funds gained 21.7%, 42.3%, 87.7%, 2.8% and 27.0%. The hedge fund managers were well paid and well informed, and their fees ate what edge they had.

### Why anyone bothers

Grossman and Stiglitz's result explains who does get paid. An analyst who spends a year learning one industry better than anyone has information that cost a year. The price can't carry it until she trades on it, so she earns something for the year. A reader of the morning paper has information that cost nothing, and the price carried it by the time the paper was printed.
