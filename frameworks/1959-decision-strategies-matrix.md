# Decision strategies matrix

Some group decisions are easy because everyone wants the same thing and everyone knows how to get it. Others fall apart because people want different things, or because nobody knows what will work. In 1959 the sociologists James D. Thompson and Arthur Tuden split these apart. They asked two questions of any decision. Do people agree on what they want? Do they agree on what causes what?

The answers give four boxes, and each box needs its own way of deciding. When people agree on both, the answer can be worked out, so the right strategy is computation. Hand it to experts and a rule book. When they agree on the goal but not on cause and effect, they need judgement. A panel of wise heads weighs the evidence and votes. When they agree on cause and effect but want different things, they need compromise. Each side bargains and gives a little. When they agree on neither, there is no method left. Someone has to offer a new vision that people can rally round, and Thompson and Tuden called this inspiration.

The trait the matrix sorts on is where the disagreement sits: in the facts, in the values or in both. Many bad decisions come from using the strategy for one box in another. Experts who treat a clash of values as a sum produce a plan that adds up and that nobody accepts. Groups that bargain over a question of fact are just as wrong. If two experts disagree on how a disease spreads, splitting the difference gives you a policy that matches neither view of the facts. Ralph Stacey later built his matrix on the same two questions.

## Examples

**Working out a pension.** A pension fund agrees on the goal, which is to pay each member what they are owed. It also agrees on the rules for how much that is. So nobody holds a debate. A clerk or a computer applies the formula, and that is computation.

**A jury.** Twelve people share one goal, which is to reach the right verdict. They differ on what the evidence shows. The law doesn't ask them to calculate. It asks them to talk it through and vote, which is a strategy of judgement.

**The Great Compromise.** At the American constitutional convention in 1787, the large and small states clashed over how to share power in Congress. They understood well enough how each voting rule would work. What they disagreed on was who should gain. The deal was a Senate with two seats per state and a House shared by population. Neither side got all it wanted, which is what compromise looks like.
