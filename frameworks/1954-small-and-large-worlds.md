# Small and large worlds

**In a small world you can list every state and every act before you choose. In a large world some bridges can only be crossed when you reach them.**

1954 · Leonard Savage · [Original book](https://archive.org/details/foundationsofsta0000sava) · Feeds 1 axis

## The idea

In 1954 the American statistician Leonard "Jimmie" Savage published *The Foundations of Statistics*. It set out how a rational person should choose when unsure. You list the possible states of the world, the acts open to you and the result of each act in each state. Then you weigh each result by how likely you think its state is. His best known case is an omelette. Five good eggs are in a bowl and a sixth may be rotten. You can break it into the bowl, break it into a saucer first or throw it away. That is two states and three acts, and every result is clear. The book became the foundation of Bayesian decision theory.

Savage was careful about where his theory applied. He used two proverbs. "Look before you leap" fits a small world, where you can list every state before you choose. "Cross that bridge when you come to it" fits a large world, where some bridges can't be seen until you reach them. He called it "ridiculous" and "preposterous" to treat every world as small. Most readers took the theory and forgot the warning. In 2009 the game theorist Ken Binmore revived it in *Rational Decisions*. He argued that people keep using Bayesian tools in large worlds, where Savage never meant them to go. The trait this sorts on is whether you can list every state of the world in advance.

## What goes wrong

This is not the same as known odds. You can list every state and still not know how likely each one is, as with a new drug that either works or doesn't. A large world is worse, because the state that matters may not be on your list at all. No amount of care with the numbers will fix that. The mistake is building a careful model of a small world and then trusting it in a large one. The model feels complete because everything on the list has been weighed.

## What to do instead

The better response is to ask what you would do if the truth turned out to be off the list. In a large world it pays to keep options open and leave slack, so you can improvise when the bridge comes.

## Examples

### Beating blackjack

Blackjack is a small world. The cards left in the deck fix every state, and the rules fix every act. In 1961 the mathematician Edward Thorp used an IBM 704 computer at MIT to work out the best play for each mix of remaining cards. He published the result that year in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* as "A favorable strategy for twenty-one". He then took $10,000 to the casinos and won $11,000 in a single weekend. His 1962 book *Beat the Dealer* sold over 700,000 copies. In a world this small, looking before you leap works.

### Apollo 13

On 13 April 1970 an oxygen tank on Apollo 13 burst. The craft was nearly 56 hours into its flight and about 200,000 miles from Earth. The three astronauts moved into the lunar module and used it as a lifeboat. It had been built to keep two men alive for two days, and now it had to keep three alive for about four. After a day and a half the carbon dioxide in the cabin began to build up. The command module had spare filters, but they were square and the lunar module's sockets were round. Engineers on the ground improvised an adapter from plastic bags, cardboard and tape, and talked the crew through building it. They splashed down safely on 17 April. Nobody had put this state on a list in advance, and the crew survived because people knew how to cross the bridge when they came to it.
