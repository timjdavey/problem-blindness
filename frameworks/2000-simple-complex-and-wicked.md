# Simple, complex and wicked

**Sorts problems by whether people agree on the problem and on the fix.**

2000 · Nancy Roberts · [Original paper](https://ipmr.net/index.php/ipmr/article/view/175) · Feeds 1 axis

## The idea

When a group of people faces a problem, they can disagree in two places. They can disagree about what the problem is, and they can disagree about how to fix it. Nancy Roberts, a professor of public policy, used these two questions in 2000 to sort public problems into three kinds.

If people agree on both, the problem is simple. Someone just has to do the work. If they agree on the problem but not the fix, it is complex, and the fight is over means. If they agree on neither, the problem is wicked. Roberts borrowed the word from Rittel and Webber, but she pinned it to one trait. People can't agree on what the problem is, so they can't agree on the fix either. The trait this sorts on is where people disagree: about the problem, about the fix or about both. The same two questions drive Thompson and Tuden's [decision strategies matrix](1959-decision-strategies-matrix.md) and the [Stacey matrix](1996-stacey-matrix.md).

Watch the word complex. For Roberts it means a fight over means. In [Cynefin](1999-cynefin.md) and for [Glouberman and Zimmerman](2002-simple-complicated-and-complex.md) it means parts that adapt to each other, so what worked last time is no guide. For [Warren Weaver](1948-simplicity-disorganised-and-organised-complexity.md) it means many parts that interact. A problem can be complex in one sense and not in the others, so say which one you mean.

## What goes wrong

The mistake is to treat a wicked problem as simple and send in experts to fix it. They will fix what they think the problem is, and half the people involved will see the fix as the problem.

## What to do instead

Roberts also named three ways of coping with wicked problems. You can hand the problem to one authority and let them decide. You can let the sides compete until one wins. Or you can bring them together to work it out. Each has a cost. Authority is quick but others may ignore the result. Competition can drag on for years, and working together is slow and needs trust. The better response is to name where the disagreement sits first, then pick the way of coping whose cost you can bear.

## Examples

### The pothole

Everyone agrees a pothole on the high street is a problem, and everyone agrees the fix is to fill it. This is simple. The council sends a crew and the only question is when.

### Childhood obesity

Most people in Britain agree that too many children are overweight. They disagree on the fix: taxing sugar, teaching cooking, building parks or leaving it to parents. That makes it complex. The UK's sugar tax on soft drinks came in from April 2018, and the argument over whether it was the right tool carried on after it.

### Drug policy

Some people see drug use as a crime, others as an illness and others as a private choice. Because they disagree on what the problem is, they can't agree on a fix. In 2001 Portugal stopped treating possession of drugs for personal use as a crime and treated it as a health matter instead. It was a choice about what the problem is, not only about how to solve it.
