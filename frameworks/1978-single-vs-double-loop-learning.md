# Single vs double-loop learning

**Single-loop learning fixes errors against a fixed goal, like a thermostat. Double-loop learning questions the goal itself.**

1978 · Chris Argyris and Donald Schön · [Original book](https://books.google.com/books/about/Organizational_Learning.html?id=2aYOAQAAMAAJ) · Feeds 1 axis

## The idea

A thermostat is set to 20 degrees. When the room gets colder it turns the heating on, and when the room warms up it turns it off. It corrects errors very well, but it can never ask whether 20 degrees is right. Chris Argyris and Donald Schön called this single-loop learning. You compare what happens with a fixed goal and adjust what you do.

Double-loop learning goes a step further. When things go wrong, you also question the goal and the beliefs behind it. Maybe the room is empty all day and shouldn't be heated at all. Argyris and Schön argued that organisations are good at the first loop and bad at the second. People get rewarded for hitting the target, not for asking if it is the right target, and questioning the goal often feels like an attack on whoever set it.

The framework sorts problems by where the error lives. If the goal is sound and the method is off, single-loop learning fixes it. If the goal itself is wrong, no amount of tuning helps.

## What goes wrong

The mistake is to keep turning the dial harder on a problem that needs a new goal. Each round of tweaks makes the team better at something that no longer matters.

## Examples

### Kodak

In 1975 Steven Sasson, an engineer at Kodak, built one of the first digital cameras. Kodak saw what was coming. A 1981 internal study warned that digital would replace film, and in 2005 Kodak sold more digital cameras in the US than anyone else. But it made most of its money from film and prints, and it kept judging digital by how well it fed that goal. It sold printer docks, photo kiosks and later ink, betting that people would still print their photos. That was single-loop learning: better ways to reach the old goal. The question it was slow to ask was how it would make money once people stopped printing at all. In January 2012 Kodak filed for bankruptcy.

### The call centre

A call centre is judged on how long each call takes, so managers train staff to keep calls short. Average call time falls, which looks like learning. A double-loop question would ask why customers are calling in the first place. Often many of the calls are people chasing something that went wrong the first time. Shorter calls make that worse, because more problems are left unfixed and more people ring back.
