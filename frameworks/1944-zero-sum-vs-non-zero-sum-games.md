# Zero-sum vs non-zero-sum games

**In a zero-sum game one side's gain is the other's loss, so you face an opponent who adapts to you.**

1944 · John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern · [Original book](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691130613/theory-of-games-and-economic-behavior) · Feeds 2 axes

## The idea

In a game of poker the money on the table stays the same. Every pound you win is a pound someone else loses, so add up all the wins and losses and you get zero. John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern called this a zero-sum game in their 1944 book *Theory of Games and Economic Behavior*. In other games the total can grow or shrink. Two firms that trade can both end up richer, and two countries in a war can both end up poorer. These are non-zero-sum games.

The trait this sorts problems on is whether the other side's interests are the exact opposite of yours. If they are, you face an opponent who is watching you and adapting. Any habit you show will be used against you, so the best plan is often to be hard to predict. If the interests overlap, the other side is partly a partner. The best plan is then to find the deal that makes the total bigger and to build enough trust to share it.

## What goes wrong

Both mistakes are common. Treat a shared game as zero-sum and you fight over a pie you could have grown, so both sides walk away with less. Treat a true contest as shared and you show your hand to someone who will use it. A third mistake is to forget the other side is a player at all. Then you plan as if they will hold still while you make your move.

## What to do instead

The better response is to ask first whether the other side gains from your loss. If it does, keep your moves hard to read. If it doesn't, look for the deal that grows the total.

## Examples

### Penalty kicks

A penalty in football is close to zero-sum, since a goal for the kicker is a goal against the keeper. If a striker always shoots to his stronger side, keepers study the footage and dive that way. The best kickers mix their choices so that no pattern shows. A study of more than a thousand penalties in European leagues found that players mix sides much as the theory predicts.

### Trade and mercantilism

For two centuries many European states saw trade as zero-sum. A nation got rich by selling more than it bought and piling up gold, so a neighbour's gain was its loss. In 1776 Adam Smith argued that trade lets both sides gain, because each does what it does best. The zero-sum view fed tariffs and trade wars that often left both sides poorer.

### The orange

Fisher and Ury tell a story in *Getting to Yes* (1981) about two sisters who both want the one orange in the house. They split it down the middle. One then eats the fruit of her half and throws away the peel, while the other uses the peel of her half for a cake. Treating the orange as a fixed pie cost each sister half of what she could have had.
