# Problem typology

Schools talk about "problem solving" as if it were one skill. David Jonassen, a professor of education, thought this was wrong. In a 2000 paper, "Toward a design theory of problem solving", he argued that fixing a car and choosing a career are such different tasks that they barely share a method. Teaching one general skill helps with none of them.

He listed eleven kinds of problem. At the tidy end are logic puzzles, algorithms and the story problems in maths books. The middle holds rule-using problems and decisions. It also holds troubleshooting, diagnosis and strategy. At the messy end are case analysis, design and dilemmas. Three traits spread them out. The first is structure, meaning how clear the goal and the rules are. The second is complexity, meaning how many things are in play and how much they affect each other. The third is how tied the problem is to its setting. A textbook problem is abstract and could sit in any book, while a real case only makes sense with knowledge of that trade and that place. Jonassen called this domain specificity.

The mistake Jonassen saw was schools teaching almost only the tidy kinds. Students learn to spot a pattern, plug in numbers and get the one right answer. Then they leave and meet problems with no single answer, where the hard part is working out what the problem is. A student trained only on tidy problems tries to find the formula. Often there isn't one. The better response is to name the kind of problem first, then use the method that suits it.

## Examples

**The maths story problem.** A textbook asks how many apples are left after Sam gives some away. Students soon learn that "left" means subtract, and they get it right without picturing any apples. They have learned to solve a type of sentence, not a type of situation, so a question worded in a new way stumps them.

**Troubleshooting a car.** Your car won't start. There is a clear goal and a limited set of likely causes, such as the battery, the fuel or the starter. You test each one in turn. A good mechanic works through these fast because they know the fault tree, and that skill is quite different from design or decision making.

**A dilemma.** A family must decide whether to move a parent with dementia into a care home. There is no right answer to find. Each choice gives up something that matters, and two loving families could choose differently. Treating this like troubleshooting, as if one hidden fact would settle it, only adds guilt.
