# Stacey matrix

Before you decide how to tackle a problem, you can ask two questions. Do the people involved agree on what should happen? And do you know what will happen if you act? Ralph Stacey, a British professor of management, drew these as two axes on a chart in the mid 1990s. It grew out of an older grid by James Thompson and Arthur Tuden that asked much the same pair of questions.

The trait it sorts on is distance from the corner where both answers are yes. Near that corner you can plan, set targets and measure progress, because everyone wants the same thing and the steps are known. Far from it, in the opposite corner, nobody agrees and nobody can predict anything. In between is a wide zone where you know a little and agree a little. Stacey called this the zone of complexity, and he argued it is where most interesting decisions live.

The mistake the matrix warns against is using the tools of the safe corner everywhere. A detailed plan assumes you know cause and effect. A vote or a top down order assumes the disagreement doesn't matter. In the middle zone both fail, and you do better with small trials, open talk and changing course as you learn. Stacey later dropped the matrix from his own work because he felt it made a messy idea look too tidy. It lives on anyway, mostly in software teams.

This framework feeds the [Shared vs contested values](../axes/shared-vs-contested-values.md) and [Transparent vs opaque causes](../axes/transparent-vs-opaque-causes.md) axes.

## Examples

**Building a bridge.** The engineers know how steel and concrete behave, and the council agrees it wants a bridge. This sits near the safe corner, so a fixed plan with a budget and a timetable is the right tool.

**HS2.** Building a high speed railway from London to the north of England is a known kind of engineering, though HS2's soaring costs showed it was less certain than the plans assumed. What people never agreed on was whether it was worth the money. The project was run as an engineering job while the argument over its worth never ended. In October 2023 the government cancelled the leg to Manchester.

**A new app.** A start-up's founders all agree they want the app to succeed, but they have no idea what users will actually do with it. Agreement is high and certainty is low. Writing a two year plan here is a waste, and shipping a small version to learn from is better.
