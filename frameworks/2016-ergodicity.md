# Ergodicity

Since the 1650s people have judged a gamble by its expected value. You list every outcome, weight each by its chance and add them up. Ole Peters, a physicist at the London Mathematical Laboratory, thought this hid an assumption that physicists check and economists rarely did. In 2016 he and the Nobel winning physicist Murray Gell-Mann set out the case in a paper on how to judge gambles. In 2019 Peters made it again for a wider audience in Nature Physics.

Imagine a coin game where heads grows your wealth by 50% and tails shrinks it by 40%. If a huge crowd plays one round, the average player gains 5%, so expected value says play. Now follow one person over many rounds. A head and a tail together multiply your wealth by 1.5 times 0.6, which is 0.9. So a typical player loses about 5% a round. The crowd average keeps rising only because a lucky few get vastly rich. When the average over a crowd matches the average for one person over time, a process is called ergodic. This game is not, and nor is most of life where gains and losses compound.

The mistake is to judge a repeated, compounding bet by its expected value. You live one path through time, not the average of a million parallel lives. A loss that wipes you out ends the game for good, however good the bet looked on paper. The better response is to ask what happens to one player who keeps playing. That means looking at the growth rate over time, and staking a share of what you have so that no single loss can finish you.

## Examples

**The coin game over 100 rounds.** Give a crowd £100 each and have them toss the coin 100 times. The expected wealth at the end is about £13,150. A player who gets 50 heads and 50 tails, the most likely split, ends with about 52p. About 86% of the crowd ends with less than they started with. The average describes almost nobody.

**Kelly betting.** In 1956 John Kelly at Bell Labs worked out how much of your money to stake on a repeated bet so that it grows fastest over time. The answer is a fixed share, never everything. In the coin game, staking a quarter of your wealth on each toss turns a losing game into one that grows about 0.6% a round. In the early 1960s Ed Thorp used Kelly's rule to size his bets at blackjack.

**Insurance.** On expected value, insurance is a puzzle. The insurer only signs if it expects to profit, so the buyer must expect to lose. In 2015 Peters and Alexander Adamou argued that the puzzle goes away once you look at growth over time. A family that a house fire could ruin grows its wealth faster by paying the premium. An insurer with far more money also grows faster by taking it, because the same loss does far more lasting harm to a small fortune than to a large one.
