# Levels of uncertainty

People say "it's uncertain" as if uncertainty came in one size. Warren Walker is a policy analyst at Delft University of Technology. In 2003 he and his colleagues argued that it comes in grades, and that you need to know which grade you face before you pick a tool. At one end the future is clear enough that a single forecast will do. Next come futures where you can list the outcomes and give them odds, like a weather forecast. Further on you can list a few plausible futures but can't rank them. At the far end you can't even list what might happen. They called this last grade total ignorance.

The same paper asked a second question. Where does the uncertainty sit? It might be in the inputs, such as how fast a population will grow. It might be in the model itself, because nobody is sure how the parts of the system connect. The paper named five places in all: the context, the model, the inputs, the parameters and the outcomes. A third question asked about the nature of the doubt. It may come from gaps in what we know, which more research could fix. Or it may come from real randomness in the world, which no research removes. That is the split in [Reducible vs irreducible uncertainty](../axes/reducible-vs-irreducible-uncertainty.md). The trait this sorts on is how deep the doubt runs and where it sits. A later version from 2013, written with Robert Lempert of the RAND Corporation and Jan Kwakkel of Delft, set out a fuller ladder of levels and tied the top ones to deep uncertainty.

The trap is to treat a high level as a low one. Analysts like probabilities, so they put odds on futures that nobody can rank and report a single number with a neat error bar. That number looks scientific but hides the chance that the model or the framing is wrong. The better response is to name the level and the location first. They tell you whether to forecast, to plan for a few scenarios or to build in the ability to change course.

This framework feeds the [Risk vs Knightian uncertainty](../axes/risk-vs-knightian-uncertainty.md) and [Reducible vs irreducible uncertainty](../axes/reducible-vs-irreducible-uncertainty.md) axes.

## Examples

**Covid-19 in March 2020.** On 16 March 2020 a team at Imperial College London published a model that projected around 510,000 deaths in Great Britain if nothing was done. The headline was one number, but the uncertainty sat in several places. The inputs were shaky, because nobody yet knew how deadly the virus was or how fast it spread. What to do next was a question of values, since it meant trading lives against jobs and schooling. Treating it all as a modelling question pushed the argument about values out of sight.

**A pension fund.** An actuary can work out how long members are likely to live from life tables. That is a low level, where the odds are known. Whether the fund's investments will hold up over fifty years sits higher. The actuary can sketch scenarios but can't rank them with any confidence. A fund that uses the same tool for both questions will be far too sure about the second.
