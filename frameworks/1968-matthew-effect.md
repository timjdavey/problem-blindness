# Matthew effect

**Early success makes later success easier, so small or lucky leads grow into large gaps.**

1968 · Robert K. Merton, Derek de Solla Price · [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.159.3810.56) · Feeds 1 axis

## The idea

In 1968 the sociologist Robert K. Merton wrote in *Science* about how credit moves in science. He drew on Harriet Zuckerman's interviews with Nobel laureates. They told her that when they wrote a paper with an unknown colleague, readers gave the credit to them. When two scientists found the same thing at the same time, the better known one got the fame. Merton named this after a line in the Gospel of Matthew: "For unto every one that hath shall be given, and he shall have abundance."

In 1976 the historian of science Derek de Solla Price turned the idea into a model he called cumulative advantage. A paper's chance of being cited again grows with the citations it already has. Run that rule for long enough and a few papers end up with most of the citations, while most get almost none.

The trait this picks out is whether what you have now changes what you get next. Where it does, results build on themselves, and the gap between two people says as much about who got lucky early as about who is better.

## What goes wrong

People read a winner's lead as proof of merit. They back the leader, fund the famous lab and copy the hit. But under cumulative advantage much of the lead came from an early break, so the next pick based on it is partly a bet on luck. The same mistake hurts the people who fall just short. They read a near miss as a verdict and stop trying.

## What to do instead

Judge people and options on what they did from the same start, not on what they have piled up. Look for a chance to compare cases that began almost alike, such as those just above and just below a cut-off. Keep entering after a near miss. If you run a contest, know that your early picks will snowball, and spread the early chances wider.

## Examples

### A music market run eight times

In 2006 Matthew Salganik, Peter Dodds and Duncan Watts set up an online music market with 48 unknown songs. 14,341 people listened to and downloaded songs. Some were split into separate worlds where they could see how often others had downloaded each song, and some saw no counts at all. Seeing the counts made the hits bigger and harder to predict. The same song could top one world and sit near the bottom of another. The best songs rarely did badly and the worst rarely did well, but anything else could happen.

### Dutch research grants

In 2018 Thijs Bol, Mathijs de Vaan and Arnout van de Rijt looked at early career grants from the Netherlands' science funder. They compared researchers who scored just above the cut-off with those who scored just below it, so the two groups were almost alike. Over the next eight years the winners gained more than twice as much funding as the near misses. Part of the gap came from the near misses no longer applying.

### Random gifts to strangers

In 2014 van de Rijt and his colleagues handed out early success at random. They gave money to some new Kickstarter projects, awards to some Wikipedia editors, signatures to some online petitions and good ratings to some product reviews. Each kind of early boost led to more success later than the control group got. But a bigger boost didn't lead to much more, so the snowball has limits.
