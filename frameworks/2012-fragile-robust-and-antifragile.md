# Fragile, robust and antifragile

**Sorts things by how they respond to shocks. Fragile things break, robust things stay the same and antifragile things get better.**

2012 · Nassim Nicholas Taleb · [Original book](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/176227/antifragile-by-nassim-nicholas-taleb/) · Feeds 5 axes

## The idea

Post a wine glass and a rubber ball across the country and only one will arrive damaged. Now think of your muscles after a hard week at the gym. They come back stronger because of the strain. Taleb sorted things by this one trait: what happens to them when they are shaken.

Fragile things are harmed by shocks, and one big shock does far more harm than many small ones. Robust things stay much the same. Antifragile things get better, up to a point, because stress makes them adapt. Taleb made up that last word because English had no word for the opposite of fragile. Bones, immune systems and industries that learn from failure all fit.

Taleb treats robust and resilient as the same thing, but they aren't. A robust thing resists the shock and barely moves, like a sea wall. A resilient thing takes the hit, bends or breaks, and then recovers, like a forest growing back after a fire. C. S. Holling drew this line in ecology in 1973 ([Resilience and stability](1973-resilience-and-stability.md)). Both come back to where they started, which is what sets them apart from antifragile things. Those come back somewhere better.

What decides which group you are in is the shape of the harm. Jumping from one metre ten times does you no harm, but one jump from ten metres can break your legs. The harm grows faster than the shock, so the curve of your payoff bends down. This is called concave, and it is what fragile means. A payoff that bends up, where one big shock helps you more than many small ones, is called convex and is antifragile. So the shape tells you whether swings hurt or help. Take two years with the same average shock. A wild year leaves a concave payoff worse off than a steady year, and a convex payoff better off. This is called Jensen's inequality: the average of the outcomes is not the outcome of the average.

## What goes wrong

The sorting matters because it changes the question you ask. Most planning tries to forecast the next shock and prepare for it. Taleb argues that in a fat-tailed world the forecast will often be wrong.

## What to do instead

Look at the shape of your exposure instead. Ask what happens to you if things go very badly or very well. If a big surprise would ruin you, you are fragile no matter how good the forecast is. Cutting that exposure is safer than sharpening the prediction.

## Examples

### Fukushima Daiichi

The nuclear plant on Japan's east coast had a sea wall built for a tsunami of under six metres. On 11 March 2011 a wave of about 14 metres arrived. It flooded the backup generators, which sat low in the buildings, and three reactors melted down. The plant was fragile to a wave bigger than its planners expected, and the harm grew far faster than the size of the wave.

### Air travel

Each crash is investigated, and the findings go into the rules and designs used by every airline. The system as a whole learns from each failure, so flying has become far safer over the decades. Taleb uses this as an example of antifragility. Each flight is fragile, but the industry gains from its shocks.

### Your savings

A household with no savings and a large mortgage is fragile, because losing one job could mean losing the house. A household with six months of cash in the bank is robust. A lost job hurts, but they keep the house and end up about where they started. A household that also keeps spare cash ready to invest is antifragile. When a crash comes they buy shares or a house cheaply, so they end up better off because of the shock. None of them needs to forecast the next recession to know which they are.

### Barbell

Taleb's advice for savings is to put most of the money, say 85 to 90%, somewhere very safe, like cash or government bonds, and the rest in a few wild bets. The safe part caps the loss, since the worst case is losing the small slice. The wild slice keeps the chance of a huge gain. Taleb argues this beats putting everything in the middle, in assets that look only a little risky, because their risk tends to hide until a crash.
