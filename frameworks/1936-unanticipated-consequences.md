# Unanticipated consequences

**Any action taken on purpose has results nobody meant, and Merton named five reasons people miss them.**

1936 · Robert K. Merton · [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.2307/2084615) · Feeds 1 axis

## The idea

In December 1936 the young American sociologist Robert Merton published a short paper in the first volume of the *American Sociological Review*. It asked why actions taken on purpose so often end somewhere nobody meant. He wasn't talking about accidents. He meant the results of doing exactly what you set out to do. Some of those results are harmful, some are welcome and some are just odd, but none were part of the plan.

Merton named five reasons people fail to see them coming. The first is ignorance, since nobody can know everything that bears on a choice. The second is error, like assuming a habit that worked before will work again. The third he called the "imperious immediacy of interest". You want the near result so badly that you never look at what else your action will do. The fourth is basic values, which can forbid an act or demand it whatever it leads to. The fifth is the self-defeating prediction, where saying what will happen changes what happens. Merton came back to that last idea in 1948 with the [self-fulfilling prophecy](1948-self-fulfilling-prophecy.md). The trait this sorts on is whether you can foresee the full set of outcomes your own action will bring about.

## What goes wrong

The mistake is to judge a plan only by whether it hits its target. The plan works, the target moves, and the harm turns up somewhere nobody was looking. Because the planners were right about the thing they cared about, they are slow to believe they caused the rest.

## What to do instead

The better response is to ask what else your action will touch before you take it, and to keep watching after you do. Merton's five causes work as a checklist. Ask what you don't know, where an old habit might mislead you, what you are too keen to look past, what your values stop you weighing and whether announcing the plan will change how people act.

## Examples

### China's war on sparrows

In 1958 China launched a campaign against four pests, which were rats, flies, mosquitoes and sparrows. The sparrows were blamed for eating grain. People banged pots until the birds dropped from the sky with exhaustion, and hundreds of millions were killed. With few sparrows left to eat them, locusts and other insects swarmed and harvests fell. The campaign was one of the causes of the Great Chinese Famine of 1959 to 1961, and in 1960 sparrows were taken off the list and replaced by bedbugs.

### Putting out every fire

After huge fires in 1910, the US Forest Service set out to stop all wildfires. In 1935 it ruled that every fire should be under control by 10 a.m. the morning after it was reported. For decades the rule looked like a success. Yet small fires had been clearing the dead wood on forest floors, and without them it piled up. When fires did break out, they burnt far hotter and spread further ([Detail vs dynamic complexity](1990-detail-vs-dynamic-complexity.md)).

### The cobra bounty

A story, often told but never confirmed, says the British rulers of Delhi once paid a reward for every dead cobra. People killed snakes and claimed the money. Then some began breeding cobras for the reward. When the government found out and scrapped the scheme, the breeders let their snakes go, and Delhi ended up with more cobras than before. In 2001 the economist Horst Siebert called this the cobra effect. Whether or not it happened, it shows the trap. A reward meant to remove a thing gave people a reason to make more of it.
