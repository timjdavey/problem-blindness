# Prospect theory

In 1979 Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky published "Prospect theory" in Econometrica. They gave students a choice between a sure 3,000 (in Israeli pounds) and an 80% chance of 4,000. Eighty percent took the sure thing. Then they flipped the signs. Now the choice was a sure loss of 3,000 or an 80% chance of losing 4,000. Ninety-two percent took the gamble, even though it loses 3,200 on average. The same people who played safe with gains gambled with losses.

The theory says people don't judge an outcome by where it leaves them. They judge it as a gain or a loss from where they stand now, which is called the reference point. Losses weigh more than gains of the same size, so a loss you can still dodge feels worth almost any risk. In the 1950s Richard McGlothlin found that people at the races bet more on long shots in the last race of the day. Most of them were down by then, and a long shot was their last chance to go home even.

The trait this sorts on is whether every option sits below where you stand, so the best on offer is still a loss. Above the reference point people are careful. Below it they gamble. The mistake is to let the person carrying the loss decide whether to keep betting, since they are the one most tempted to double up. The better response is to set the limit on losses before anything goes wrong, and to judge each option against what doing nothing would cost rather than against getting back to even.

## Examples

**Nick Leeson and Barings.** From 1992 Nick Leeson traded futures for Barings Bank in Singapore. When his trades lost money he hid the losses in an error account numbered 88888 and placed bigger bets to win them back. On 17 January 1995 an earthquake hit Kobe and the Japanese stock market fell. Leeson bet heavily that it would recover, and it kept falling. By the time he fled in February the losses came to £827 million, more than the bank's capital. Barings, which had stood since 1762, was sold to ING for £1. Each step was a gamble to escape a sure loss, and each one made the loss bigger.

**Chasing losses at the casino.** Most people have watched someone down for the night keep playing to get back to where they started. They would never have bet that much at the start of the evening. The money already lost changed where they felt they stood, so a big bet now looked like the way home rather than a fresh risk.
