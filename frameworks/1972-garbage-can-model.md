# Garbage can model

**In many organisations problems, solutions, people and chances to decide drift about on their own and get matched by timing more than by logic.**

1972 · Michael Cohen, James March and Johan Olsen · [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.2307/2392088) · Feeds 1 axis

## The idea

The textbook picture of a decision is neat. An organisation spots a problem, lists the options, weighs them and picks the best. Michael Cohen, James March and Johan Olsen thought universities didn't work like that. Their goals were vague and often clashed. Nobody fully understood how the work got done. People drifted in and out of each decision as their time allowed. The three called places like this organised anarchies. In 1972 they published "A garbage can model of organizational choice" in Administrative Science Quarterly. It included a computer simulation, written in Fortran, of how such a place makes choices.

In their model four streams flow more or less on their own. The streams are problems, solutions, people with limited time and chances to decide. A chance to decide might be a meeting, a budget round or a hire. Each chance to decide is a garbage can. People dump into it whatever problems and solutions happen to be lying about at the time. In their words, an organisation is "a collection of choices looking for problems, issues and feelings looking for decision situations in which they might be aired, solutions looking for issues to which they might be an answer, and decision makers looking for work". In the simulation many choices got made without solving the problem they were meant for. Some were made before the problems arrived. In others the problems left for another meeting before anyone chose. The trait this sorts on is whether the tool was picked for the problem or the problem was picked for the tool.

## What goes wrong

The law of the instrument is one person with a hammer. The garbage can is a whole organisation with one. A team that has built a system, a vendor with a product or a leader with a pet idea will go looking for problems it can answer. The problem then gets described in the tool's terms, so the fit looks good on paper. The mistake is to take a match between a problem and a solution as proof that the solution fits. It may only mean they turned up at the same meeting.

## What to do instead

The better response is to check the order. Ask whether the problem was defined before the solution was on the table, and whether anyone looked at other options. If the solution came first, test it hard against the shape of the problem or start again from the problem.

## Examples

### IBM Watson at MD Anderson

In February 2011 IBM's Watson beat the Jeopardy! champions Ken Jennings and Brad Rutter on American television. IBM then went looking for work for it, and health care became a main target. The MD Anderson Cancer Center in Houston built an "Oncology Expert Advisor" on Watson. Along the way its focus switched from leukaemia to lung cancer. An audit by the University of Texas System, made public in early 2017, found MD Anderson had spent about $62 million and that the system was not in clinical use. In January 2022 IBM sold much of its Watson Health business to the private equity firm Francisco Partners.

### The ASX and the blockchain

CHESS has settled share trades on the Australian Securities Exchange since 1994. In February 2015 the ASX said it would replace its main trading and settlement systems. In January 2016 it paid A$14.9 million for a 5% stake in Digital Asset, a US blockchain firm, and picked it to build the new settlement system. Its own press release said a final decision on the technology would come in 2017. In December 2017 the ASX confirmed it would replace CHESS with Digital Asset's technology. In November 2022, after a review by Accenture, it scrapped the project and wrote off A$245 to 255 million. Its chairman pointed to "significant technology, governance and delivery challenges".
