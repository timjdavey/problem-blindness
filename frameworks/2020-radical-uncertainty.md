# Radical uncertainty

**Many real problems can't be given probabilities at all, because we can't list what might happen.**

2020 · John Kay and Mervyn King · [Original book](https://wwnorton.com/books/9781324004776) · Feeds 2 axes

## The idea

Some uncertainty can be priced. A roulette wheel has 37 slots, so you can work out the odds of every bet. Kay and King argue that most big decisions in life, business and policy are nothing like this. You can't list everything that might happen, so you can't give each outcome a probability. They call this radical uncertainty, and it is close to what Frank Knight meant by uncertainty in 1921. The trait this sorts on is whether you can list what might happen and put odds on it.

Kay is an economist, and King was governor of the Bank of England through the 2008 crisis. They borrow an idea from the statistician Jimmie Savage, who split problems into small worlds and large worlds. In a small world, like a game of cards, all the outcomes are known and the maths works. In a large world, like the real economy, new things keep happening that nobody had listed.

## What goes wrong

Economists, they argue, keep building small world models and applying them to large world problems. Putting a precise probability on a large world event gives false comfort. It can lead to worse choices than honest doubt would.

## What to do instead

Their advice is to stop making up numbers. When the odds can't be known, ask "what is going on here?" Build the best story you can that explains the facts, test it against what others think and choose actions that would survive if the story is wrong.

## Examples

### The bin Laden raid

In 2011 President Obama had to decide whether to raid a compound in Abbottabad, Pakistan, where Osama bin Laden might be hiding. His advisers each gave a probability that bin Laden was there, and their numbers differed widely. Obama treated it as, in effect, a coin toss. Kay and King use this case to show that the numbers added little. What mattered was judging what was going on and what would happen if they were wrong.

### The Queen's question

In November 2008 the Queen visited the London School of Economics and asked why nobody had seen the financial crisis coming. The models used by banks and central banks put tiny odds on such an event. They were small world tools in a large world, because the crisis grew from links between banks that the models left out.

### Choosing a career

At eighteen you can't list the jobs that will exist when you are forty. Nobody can give you the odds that a career will still pay well in twenty years. People make good choices anyway by asking what is going on in a field and keeping their options open.
