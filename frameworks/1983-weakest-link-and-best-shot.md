# Weakest link and best shot

**How much a group gets depends on how its efforts add up, as well as on how hard each member tries.**

1983 · Jack Hirshleifer · [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00141070) · Feeds 1 axis

## The idea

Economists had long said that people do too little for a good they share, like a sea wall or clean air. Each person leaves the work to the others, so too little gets made. In 1983 the UCLA economist Jack Hirshleifer pointed out in *Public Choice* that this assumes the shared good is the sum of what everyone puts in. Often it isn't.

He told a story about an island called Anarchia. It is perfectly round, and each family owns a wedge of land running from the centre to the sea. There is no government, so each family decides how high to build the sea wall on its own stretch of shore. When the sea rises, it pours in at the lowest stretch and floods everyone. So the island is only as safe as its lowest wall. Hirshleifer called this a weakest-link good. At the other extreme is a best-shot good, where only the highest effort counts, as when several groups race to make one discovery. Between the two sits the ordinary case, summation, where every bit of effort adds to the total.

The way the parts combine changes how people behave. Under weakest link, building above the lowest wall is wasted, but building below it floods you too, so families tend to match each other and free riding is mild. Under best shot, only one effort counts, so only the person who gains most bothers and free riding is at its worst. In 1989 Glenn Harrison and Hirshleifer tested this in the lab. The ranking held when people took turns, and held in part when they chose at the same time. The trait this sorts on is how the parts combine into the whole: by their sum, by their worst or by their best.

## What goes wrong

The mistake is to judge a group or a system by its average part. When the whole is set by its weakest part, a strong average hides the one part that sinks it, and effort spent anywhere else is wasted. Every part then rides on the weakest, so a whole with many parts is really one bet. When the whole is set by its best part, the mistake runs the other way. Everyone waits for someone else to make the effort, and the good may never get made.

## What to do instead

The better response is to ask how the parts add up before deciding where to spend. Where the weakest part sets the result, find it and raise it, since raising any other part does nothing. Where the best part sets it, make sure someone with a strong reason to act is on the job, since everyone else will wait.

## Examples

### The O-ring

On 28 January 1986 the space shuttle Challenger broke apart 73 seconds after launch. A rubber seal called an O-ring had failed in the cold, while thousands of other parts worked. In 1993 the economist Michael Kremer named a theory of growth after it. He modelled production as a chain of tasks where a mistake in any one can wipe out most of the product's value. In that world, firms put skilled workers with other skilled workers, since a star is wasted next to someone who botches their step. Small differences in skill then make large differences in output and pay. Kremer argued this helps explain why rich and poor countries differ so much.

### Amdahl's law

In the 1960s many hoped that linking lots of processors together would make computers far faster. In 1967 the computer designer Gene Amdahl argued against it. Part of every job has to run one step after another, and extra processors can't speed that part up. However many processors you add, the job can't finish faster than its step-by-step part takes. The idea became known as Amdahl's law. It is the weakest link in time, since the slowest part sets the pace for all the rest.
