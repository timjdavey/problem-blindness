# Simplicity, disorganised complexity and organised complexity

Think about a game of snooker. If you know how hard the cue ball was struck and where it hit the red, you can work out where both balls will go. There are only a few things to track, and the rules linking them are exact. In a 1948 essay called "Science and Complexity", Warren Weaver called these problems of simplicity. Most physics up to 1900 was about problems like this.

Now picture a table with a million balls bouncing about. You can't follow any one ball, but you can say a lot about the average. You can say how often a ball hits a cushion, or how far a ball travels before it hits another. Weaver called these problems of disorganised complexity. They have huge numbers of parts that act more or less at random, so the quirks cancel out and statistics works.

Weaver's real point was about the large middle ground. Here there are too many parts to track one by one, but too few to average away, and the parts are linked in ways that matter. A living cell, a firm or a city works like this. He called these problems of organised complexity, and he said science had barely started on them. The trait the framework sorts on is how many parts a problem has and how much they shape each other. The big mistake is to take an organised problem and treat it as one of the other two. You either pick a few variables and ignore the rest, or you average over parts whose links are the whole story.

## Examples

**A telephone exchange.** Weaver's own example was a large telephone exchange. It can't know who will ring whom or when. It can predict how many calls will come in each hour and how many lines it needs to carry them. Each caller has their own reasons, but across a whole city those reasons average out, so statistics is the right tool.

**Planning cities.** In 1961 Jane Jacobs ended *The Death and Life of Great American Cities* with a chapter on what kind of problem a city is. She used Weaver's terms. She argued that planners had treated cities as simple problems, like matching housing to jobs, or as statistical ones built on averages. A city is organised complexity. The life of a street depends on how its shops, homes and people feed each other. Tear out one part and you break the links.

**An economy.** A central bank that treats the economy as a few dials, such as rates and prices, is treating it as simple. One that relies on national averages is treating it as disorganised. Neither view sees the banks, firms and households whose links can turn a small shock into a crisis.
