# Market for lemons

**When one side of a deal knows more than the other, the deals on offer are not a fair sample.**

1970 · George Akerlof · [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.2307/1879431) · Feeds 1 axis

## The idea

In the late 1960s George Akerlof, a young economist at Berkeley, wrote a short paper about used cars. Three journals turned it down. The American Economic Review said it was trivial. The Journal of Political Economy said that if it were right, economics would be different. The Quarterly Journal of Economics took it, and in August 1970 it came out as "The market for 'lemons': quality uncertainty and the market mechanism". In 2001 Akerlof shared the Nobel prize for it.

The argument is simple. Some used cars are good and some are lemons. The seller knows which one they have, but the buyer can't tell. So the buyer will only pay the price of an average car. At that price the owners of good cars are better off keeping them, so they drop out. The cars left on sale are worse than average, so buyers offer less, and more good cars drop out. In the worst case the market shrinks until only lemons are left. When one side of a deal knows more than the other, we call it asymmetric information. When it means the worst cases are the ones that turn up, we call it adverse selection.

## What goes wrong

The trait this sorts on is whether the other side of the deal knows more than you about what is on offer. If they do, the cases that reach you are not a fair sample, because the people who know more chose whether to send them. The mistake is to judge an offer as if it had come to you at random. An AI model makes the mistake by default. It takes the claims in a CV, a listing or a prompt at face value, and never asks who chose to send them.

## What to do instead

The better response is to ask why this is on offer to you, and to get the other side to prove what they know. A warranty does this, since only a seller who trusts the car can afford to give one.

## Examples

### Harvard's health plan

In 1995 Harvard University changed how it paid for its staff's health insurance. It used to cover most of the cost of the most generous plan. Now it paid a fixed sum, and staff who wanted the generous plan paid the whole extra cost themselves. Staff knew their own health far better than the insurer did. The young and healthy moved to cheaper plans, so the people left in the generous plan were sicker and its price went up. That drove out the next healthiest group. Within three years the generous plan was gone. David Cutler and Sarah Reber told the story in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 1998.

### Job market signalling

In August 1973 Michael Spence published "Job market signaling" in the same journal. An employer can't see how able a job seeker is, but the job seeker can. Spence showed that a degree can be worth getting even if it teaches nothing useful. It works as proof because it costs an able person less effort to earn than it costs anyone else. He shared the 2001 Nobel prize with Akerlof and Joseph Stiglitz, who showed the other side of the same trick. An insurer can offer a cheap plan with a high excess, and the low risk customers pick it, so their choice tells the insurer what they know.
