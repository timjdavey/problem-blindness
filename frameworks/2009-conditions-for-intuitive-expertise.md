# Conditions for intuitive expertise

**Intuition can be trusted only when the setting is regular enough to learn and the expert has had long practice with quick, clear feedback.**

2009 · Daniel Kahneman and Gary Klein · [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016755) · Feeds 4 axes

## The idea

Daniel Kahneman spent his career showing how often expert judgement fails. Gary Klein spent his showing how well fire commanders and nurses decide under pressure. In 2009 they wrote a paper together to settle the argument. They subtitled it "a failure to disagree", because once they asked when intuition works they found they mostly agreed.

Their answer has two parts. First, the setting must be regular enough to learn. There have to be real cues that predict what will happen, the way smoke and heat tell a firefighter how a fire will behave. Second, the expert must have had long practice in that setting with quick, clear feedback. Intuition is pattern matching, so it can only be as good as the patterns the person has had a chance to learn. The trait this sorts on is whether the task lets intuition be learned.

## What goes wrong

When either part is missing, you get experts who feel sure and are often wrong. Confidence is a poor guide, because people feel just as sure in settings where there is nothing to learn.

## What to do instead

The test is not how confident the expert is. It is whether the task gave them a fair chance to learn. A surgeon's hands may deserve trust while the same surgeon's guess about whether to operate does not.

## Examples

### The fire in the basement

Klein interviewed a fire lieutenant whose crew was spraying water on what looked like a simple kitchen fire. The fire didn't respond, and the room was oddly hot and quiet. He ordered everyone out without knowing why. Moments later the floor collapsed, because the real fire was in the basement below. Years of fires with clear outcomes had taught him a pattern he couldn't put into words.

### Anaesthetists and radiologists

Kahneman and Klein contrast these two medical jobs. An anaesthetist sees the effect of each decision within minutes, because the patient's vital signs change in front of them. A radiologist often never learns whether a reading was right, since the outcome comes months later or not at all. The first job builds good intuition far more easily than the second.

### Political pundits

Philip Tetlock tracked thousands of forecasts by hundreds of experts on politics and economics over about twenty years. On long range questions they did little better than chance. World politics has few repeated cases and feedback that arrives years late, so neither condition holds.
