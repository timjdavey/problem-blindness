# Use of knowledge in society

In the 1930s and 1940s economists argued over whether a central planner could run an economy as well as a market. Oskar Lange said a planning board could work out the right prices. Friedrich Hayek, then at the London School of Economics, replied in September 1945 with a short paper in the *American Economic Review* called "The Use of Knowledge in Society". His point was that the facts a planner needs are never in one place. They are spread in small pieces across millions of people, and much of it is "the knowledge of the particular circumstances of time and place". Hayek won the Nobel prize in economics in 1974.

Hayek split knowledge into two kinds. General knowledge is the kind in textbooks, like how to grow wheat. Local knowledge is about here and now. His examples are a shipper who earns a living by filling the empty space on tramp steamers, an estate agent whose knowledge is almost all of passing chances and a trader who profits from small price gaps between towns. He argued that the economic problem is mostly "rapid adaptation to changes in the particular circumstances of time and place", so decisions should be left to the people who know those circumstances. Prices pass on the rest. If tin gets scarce its price rises, and users everywhere cut back without needing to know why. In 1998 James C. Scott pushed the idea further in *Seeing Like a State*. He called the practical knowledge built up by working in one place metis, from the Greek, and showed how grand state schemes failed by ignoring it. The trait this sorts on is whether the answer needs general knowledge or local knowledge of the particular circumstances of time and place.

The mistake is trusting general knowledge where local facts decide the answer. A planner at the centre knows the theory and the averages, but not what is on the shelf in a given shop today. An AI model trained on general text has the same blind spot. It knows a great deal about how things usually go and nothing about this firm's stock levels or this morning's prices. Local facts are often easy to write down, so this is not the same as tacit know-how. The trouble is not that the fact can't be put into words. It is that nobody at the centre has it. The better response is to ask whose facts the answer rests on. Then either hand the decision to the person on the spot, or get their facts to the decision before you make it.

## Examples

**Bali's water temples.** For centuries Balinese rice farmers set their planting dates through a network of water temples. Each group of farms is called a subak. Neighbouring subaks planted and flooded their fields together, so pests lost their food across a wide area at the same time. When the Green Revolution reached Bali in 1971, the government handed out new rice with fertiliser and pesticide and told farmers to "plant as often as possible". The shared schedule fell apart. By 1974 field workers were reporting "chaos in water scheduling" and "explosions of rice pests". In 1983 the anthropologist Stephen Lansing began to study the temples, and with the ecologist James Kremer he built a computer model of 172 subaks along two rivers. It showed the temple schedule beat planting whenever you liked. The planners had the general science right. What they lacked was who planted when, and that knowledge lived in the temples. In 2012 UNESCO made the subak system a World Heritage site.

**Walmart and Hurricane Frances.** In late August 2004 Hurricane Frances was heading for Florida. A week before it hit, Walmart's chief information officer Linda Dillman asked her staff to forecast demand from what stores had sold when Hurricane Charley struck a few weeks earlier. The data from its 3,600 American stores showed that strawberry Pop-Tarts sold at about seven times their normal rate before a hurricane, and that the top seller was beer. Nobody at head office could have guessed that from general knowledge. Trucks of both went down Interstate 95 to stores in the storm's path, and most of the stock sold quickly. The centre got it right because it pulled the facts of time and place in from the tills before it decided.
