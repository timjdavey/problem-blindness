# Law of requisite variety

W. Ross Ashby was an English psychiatrist who became one of the founders of cybernetics, the study of how systems steer and control themselves. From 1947 to 1959 he ran research at Barnwood House, a mental hospital in Gloucester. In 1952 he set out his ideas on how a brain adapts in *Design for a Brain*. In 1956 he published *An Introduction to Cybernetics*, the first textbook on the subject. In it he stated a law that he summed up as "only variety can destroy variety".

Think of a goalkeeper. Each kind of shot needs a different save. A keeper who only knows one dive will stop the shots that suit it and let the rest in. Ashby called the number of different states a thing can be in its variety. He called anything that tries to keep an outcome steady a regulator. A thermostat is one, and so is a manager. A regulator can only cut down the variety of outcomes by meeting it with variety of its own, so it needs at least as many responses as there are disturbances. Whatever it can't match leaks through to the result. In 1970 Ashby and Roger Conant went further and argued that every good regulator of a system must be a model of that system. The management thinker Stafford Beer later restated the law as "variety absorbs variety". The trait this sorts on is whether the range of situations you face is wider than the range of responses you have.

This is the law of the instrument seen from the controller's side. A person with one hammer has a variety of one. Faced with many problem shapes, they either bend every case to fit the hammer or let the misfits fail. The mistake is to judge a tool by how well it does on the cases it suits and ignore the cases it doesn't. The better response comes in two forms. You can add variety to the response, with more tools, people trained in different ways or more freedom for those close to the problem. Or you can cut the variety you face, by narrowing the job or splitting it so each part sees fewer kinds of case.

## Examples

**HIV drugs.** AZT became the first approved HIV drug in the United States in 1987. Taken alone, it worked only for a while. On 31 March 1989 a paper in Science showed that HIV from patients on long courses of AZT had become less sensitive to it. In March 1996 Alan Perelson, David Ho and their colleagues estimated that an infected person makes about 10 billion new virus particles a day. With that many copies, a mutant that dodges any one drug turns up fast. A mutant that dodges three different drugs at once is far less likely. In 1996 doctors began giving three drugs together, and results were presented at the international AIDS conference in Vancouver that year. In the United States the death rate from HIV fell 47% from 1996 to 1997. Doctors had matched the variety of the virus with variety of their own.

**One camouflage for every place.** In June 2004 the US Army adopted the Universal Camouflage Pattern. It was meant to hide a soldier equally well in every setting, from desert sand to green forest to city streets. Army scientists at Natick tested it against other patterns in 2006 and 2009, and it did worse in several settings. Soldiers in Afghanistan complained that it made them easy to spot. In 2009 Congress told the Pentagon to give troops in Afghanistan a pattern suited to the terrain, and from 2010 they wore MultiCam instead. In 2012 The Daily put the cost of the universal pattern at about $5 billion. The Army said its next step would be "a family of patterns rather than just one pattern". The universal pattern was retired on 1 October 2019.
