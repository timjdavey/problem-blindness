# Super wicked problems

**Wicked problems with four extra traits. Time is running out, and the people trying to solve it are also causing it.**

2007 (2012) · Kelly Levin, Benjamin Cashore, Graeme Auld and Steven Bernstein · [Key paper](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11077-012-9151-0) · Feeds 3 axes

## The idea

Rittel and Webber's wicked problems have no clear definition and no end point. Levin, Cashore, Auld and Bernstein argued that some wicked problems are worse still. They first presented the idea in 2007 and published the full version in 2012. A super wicked problem has four extra traits. Time is running out, so delay makes the problem harder or impossible to fix. The people trying to solve it are also the ones causing it. There is no central authority with the power to act, or the one that exists is weak. Finally, policies discount the future, so today's costs always seem to matter more than tomorrow's harms.

## What goes wrong

Together these traits create what the authors call a tragedy. Everyone can see the problem and most people want it solved. Yet each actor has a reason to wait, and nobody can force the others to move. Normal policy tools assume a government that can set a rule and enforce it, with time to learn from its mistakes. A super wicked problem takes away the power to enforce and the time to learn.

## What to do instead

Their answer is to stop hoping for one big fix. Instead you look for small steps that are hard to reverse and that build support for themselves over time. Once a policy creates jobs or habits that depend on it, it tends to stick. This is called path dependence. The aim is to bind our future selves, who will always be tempted to put things off.

## Examples

### Climate change

This is the case the authors built the idea around. Every country emits carbon, so every country is part of the cause. There is no world government to set limits. The Kyoto Protocol of 1997 relied on countries choosing to take part, and the US never ratified it. Canada pulled out in 2011 when it was clear it would miss its target.

### The UK Climate Change Act

In 2008 Britain passed a law with a legal target to cut emissions by 80% by 2050. It also set up an independent committee and a series of five year carbon budgets. This is the kind of sticky step the authors had in mind. Each budget sets a path that later governments find hard to drop, and in 2019 the target was tightened to net zero.

### Antibiotic resistance

The doctors and farmers who overuse antibiotics are the same people who need them to keep working. Each course given today helps a patient now and makes resistance a little more likely later. Time is short too, because each year of overuse breeds more resistant bugs and few new drugs are coming through. No single body controls antibiotic use across the world, so the problem has all four traits.
