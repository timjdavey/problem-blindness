# Satisficing

**Real people and firms rarely search for the best answer. They set a bar for good enough and take the first option that clears it.**

1956 · Herbert Simon · [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0042769) · Feeds 3 axes

## The idea

Economics in the 1950s pictured people as perfect optimisers. A buyer was meant to weigh every option and pick the best. Herbert Simon thought this was wrong about how people work and wrong about what they could ever do. In 1956 he published a paper in Psychological Review called "Rational choice and the structure of the environment". In it he coined a word by joining "satisfy" and "suffice". He called it satisficing.

A satisficer sets a bar for good enough, looks at options one at a time and stops at the first one that clears it. If nothing clears the bar for a long time, they lower it. Simon argued this isn't laziness. Time, attention and computing power are limited, so searching for the very best costs more than it is worth in most settings. He called this bounded rationality, and it helped win him the Nobel prize in economics in 1978. The trait this sorts on is whether good enough is fine or whether the last bit of quality matters.

## What goes wrong

The mistake runs both ways. Some people optimise where it doesn't pay, like spending a week comparing kettles. Others settle for good enough where the best answer matters, like a rough guess at a drug dose.

## What to do instead

The better response is to ask what the gap between good and best is worth, and what it costs to close it, before you decide how hard to search.

## Examples

### Fire commanders

In the 1980s Gary Klein set out to study how fire commanders compare their options under pressure. He found they mostly didn't compare at all. They spotted a familiar pattern, pictured the first plan that came to mind playing out, and used it if it seemed to work. Only if it failed that mental test did they think of a second plan. That is satisficing done well, by people with years of practice in a setting they know.

### Job hunting graduates

In 2006 Sheena Iyengar, Rachael Wells and Barry Schwartz followed students from eleven American colleges as they looked for their first job. The students who tried hardest to find the best job got starting salaries about 20% higher. Yet they felt worse about the jobs they took and about the search itself. The best answer paid more, but the hunt for it had its own cost.
