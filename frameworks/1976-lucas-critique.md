# Lucas critique

**Patterns in past economic data break once policy is built on them, because people change how they act when the rules change.**

1976 · Robert Lucas · [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0167-2231%2876%2980003-6) · Feeds 1 axis

## The idea

A bridge engineer can test steel in a lab and trust the result on site. Steel doesn't know it is in a bridge. Economists wanted the same deal. They took decades of data, found stable patterns and then used those patterns to predict what a new policy would do. Robert Lucas pointed out in 1976 that people are not steel. The patterns in the data came from how people behaved under the old rules. Change the rules and people change how they act, so the old patterns no longer hold.

The framework sorts problems by whether the thing you are modelling will respond to the model being used. A tide table doesn't change the tides. A model of how people spend or save becomes useless the moment the government acts on it in a way people notice. The fix Lucas proposed was to build models from things a new policy shouldn't change, like what people want and what firms are able to make. On top of that you model how people form their expectations, so the model can work out how they will react to the new rules.

## What goes wrong

The mistake is to treat a model of people as if it were a law of nature. It fits the past well, so you trust it to guide a big change. But the big change is exactly what breaks it. Lucas is close to [Goodhart](1975-goodharts-law.md), though his focus is the forecaster's model rather than a target.

## What to do instead

The better response is to ask what in your model would change once people know about the policy, and to build on what wouldn't.

## Examples

### The Phillips curve

In 1958 the economist Bill Phillips found that across nearly a century of British data, years of low unemployment were years of fast wage rises. In 1960 Paul Samuelson and Robert Solow suggested that the United States could choose a point on that curve. It could accept a little more inflation in exchange for fewer people out of work. Through the late 1960s American policy leaned that way. But workers and firms came to expect rising prices and built them into wages. By the 1970s America had high inflation and high unemployment at once, which the old curve said could not happen.

### The window tax

In 1696 England began taxing houses by the number of their windows. Anyone who forecast the revenue from a count of windows made in 1695 would have guessed too high. Once the tax came in, people bricked up windows to pay less. The forecast was built on how people behaved before the tax, and the tax was what changed their behaviour.

### The weekly sale

A shop sees sales jump whenever it cuts prices for a weekend, so it starts running a sale every week. Soon shoppers learn to wait for the discount. The jump the shop saw in its data came from surprise, and the new policy removed the surprise.
