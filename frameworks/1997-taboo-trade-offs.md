# Taboo trade-offs

**People trade time against money every day without a second thought. Trading money against a child's life feels wrong even to consider.**

1997 · Alan Fiske and Philip Tetlock · [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.1111/0162-895X.00058) · Feeds 2 axes

## The idea

In 1997 Alan Fiske and Philip Tetlock published "Taboo trade-offs" in Political Psychology. Fiske had sorted human relationships into a few basic kinds, from sharing within a family to buying and selling in a market. Tetlock studied how people cope when their values clash. Together they argued that in most cultures people treat some trade-offs as routine and others as taboo, like putting a price on your children, your friends or your loyalty to your country. In 2000 Tetlock and four colleagues tested this in "The psychology of the unthinkable" in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. They defined a sacred value as one that a community treats as having infinite worth, so it must never be weighed against money or other everyday goods.

In one study 228 people read about Robert, who ran a hospital. He could spend $1,000,000 on a liver transplant for a five year old called Johnny, or spend it on equipment and salaries. When Robert chose the equipment and salaries after long thought, people rated him 5.71 for outrage on a scale of 1 to 7, the worst of any version. When he saved Johnny quickly, he scored 1.51. Even thinking hard about the trade-off counted against him.

The trait this sorts on is whether every value at stake can be priced in one currency, or whether some are sacred. This is different from contested values, where people disagree about what a good result is. Here everyone can agree that a child's life is sacred and still face a budget. Tetlock noted that sacred status is what people say, not always what they do, since real budgets put limits on life all the time.

## What goes wrong

Weighted-sum methods break on sacred values. Cost benefit analysis and scoring grids both need every value turned into one number. A sacred value either gets an infinite weight and swamps everything else, or it gets a finite weight that people read as an insult.

The mistake runs both ways. One is to price the sacred value out loud and expect people to accept the sum because the maths is sound. The other is to refuse to trade at all, which hides the trade-off rather than removing it, since the money still runs out somewhere.

## What to do instead

The better response is to admit the trade-off and frame it in terms that respect the sacred value. In the same study people accepted a choice between two sick boys, a tragic trade-off of life against life, and they judged Robert better when he took his time over it.

## Examples

### The Cancer Drugs Fund

In England NICE judges new drugs by what they cost for each year of healthy life they buy. A drug that costs more than about £30,000 for each year of healthy life it buys is usually turned down, though the limit rises to about £50,000 near the end of life. Refusals of cancer drugs drew campaigns by charities and regular press stories about single patients. In October 2010 the government set up a fund to pay for cancer drugs outside those rules. By March 2015 it had cost £968 million and approved drugs for 74,380 patients. Of the patients it helped from April 2013 to March 2015, 51% got drugs that NICE had looked at and turned down. The National Audit Office found no data to show whether the fund helped patients, and noted that its overspend left less money for other services. The trade-off didn't go away. It moved out of sight, onto patients with other illnesses. In July 2016 a new fund began with its budget fixed at £340 million.

### Juries and cost benefit analysis

In 2000 Kip Viscusi gave almost 500 people eligible for jury service a car accident case to judge. In some versions the carmaker had done a cost benefit analysis and decided a safety fix wasn't worth it. Even a sound analysis did not make punitive damages any less likely. When the firm used a higher value for a human life, as government agencies do, the penalty went up rather than down. Viscusi put part of this down to anchoring, since a big number in the firm's own papers gave jurors a big number to start from. Either way the firm that priced life out loud was punished for it.
