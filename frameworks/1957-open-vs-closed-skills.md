# Open vs closed skills

In 1957 the British psychologist E. C. Poulton wrote a paper called "On prediction in skilled movements". He asked what a skilled person has to predict while they move. A gymnast on the floor knows the mat will still be there when they land. A tennis player returning a serve has to guess where the ball will be in half a second, then hit it there.

Poulton called the first kind of skill closed and the second open. In a closed skill the world is still and predictable, so you can plan the movement in advance and repeat it the same way each time. In an open skill the world keeps changing while you act, so you have to keep looking and adjust as you go. The trait this sorts on is whether the world waits for you.

The mistake is to train or judge an open skill as if it were closed. A player who has only practised one perfect serve against an empty court has learnt a routine, not how to read an opponent. The better response is to match practice to the skill. For a closed skill, repeat the same movement until it is the same every time. For an open skill, practise against many changing conditions, and train the eye to read early cues of what is about to happen.

The motor learning researcher Ann Gentile later turned the split into a grid. She asked two questions about the setting. Is it still or moving while you act? And does it change from one attempt to the next? A billiards shot is still while you play it, but each shot starts from a different place on the table. That second question picks out skills that look closed but still need varied practice.

## Examples

**Golf against tennis.** A golf drive is the textbook closed skill. The ball sits on the tee and waits, so the player can take as long as they like and swing the same way every time. A tennis return is the textbook open skill. The ball, the wind and the opponent are all moving, and the player who waits to see exactly where the ball goes is too late. The two sports reward different practice. Golfers groove one swing on the range. Tennis players drill against a partner who varies every shot.

**A billiards table.** Gentile's grid shows why closed is not one thing. Hitting the same putt from the same spot again and again is still, and the same every time. A billiards shot is also still while you play it, but the balls sit somewhere new for every shot. So a player who has only drilled one set position has practised a skill they will never use.
