# Reflexivity

**In markets, what people believe changes the facts, and the new facts then change what people believe.**

1987 · George Soros · [Original book](https://www.wiley.com/en-us/The+Alchemy+of+Finance%2C+2nd+Edition-p-9780471445494) · Feeds 1 axis

## The idea

Most forecasting assumes the thing you forecast sits still while you study it. George Soros, the Hungarian-born hedge fund manager, argued that markets are different. What investors believe about a company changes its share price, and the share price then changes the company's real prospects. A firm with a high price can raise money cheaply and buy rivals, so the belief helps make itself true. Soros called this two-way loop reflexivity, and set it out in The Alchemy of Finance.

The trait it sorts on is whether people's views of a situation are part of the situation. Where they are, there is no fixed truth for a forecaster to find. Unlike a [self-fulfilling prophecy](1948-self-fulfilling-prophecy.md), which makes itself true once and stops, this loop keeps running. Beliefs move the facts, the facts move the beliefs and round it goes. Prices don't settle around a true value. They can run far from any sensible value and stay there for years, because the beliefs and the facts keep holding each other up. Once the gap grows too large, the loop reverses and the fall feeds on itself just as the rise did.

## What goes wrong

Treat a reflexive problem as a fixed one and you trust models built on past prices. You also read a boom as proof that the fundamentals are strong, when the boom may be what made them look strong.

## What to do instead

Soros made his money by reading the loop instead. He would often ride a boom while the beliefs were still feeding it, then bet against it once it had run too far and was about to break. The better response is to ask whether beliefs are moving the facts you measure, and to watch for the point where the loop turns.

## Examples

### US housing in the 2000s

Banks lent more because house prices were rising. The extra lending pushed prices up further, which made the loans look safe and the houses look like good security for even more loans. When prices started to fall in 2006 and 2007, the same loop ran backwards. Falling prices made loans go bad, so banks lent less and prices fell further.

### Black Wednesday

In 1992 Britain was trying to keep the pound inside a fixed band against the German mark. Soros bet heavily that it would fail. Each large sale made the band harder to defend, which led other traders to sell too. On 16 September 1992 the government raised interest rates and spent its reserves, then gave up and left the band that evening. Soros's fund was said to have made about $1 billion. The belief that the pound would fall was part of what made it fall.

## Further reading

- [The Alchemy of Finance](https://www.wiley.com/en-us/The+Alchemy+of+Finance%2C+2nd+Edition-p-9780471445494), George Soros, *Wiley*, 1987. The book that set out reflexivity, linked here in its second edition.
- [Reflexivity (social theory)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflexivity_%28social_theory%29) on Wikipedia, which covers Soros's use of the term alongside older ones in sociology.
- [Fallibility, reflexivity, and the human uncertainty principle](https://www.georgesoros.com/2014/01/13/fallibility-reflexivity-and-the-human-uncertainty-principle-2/), George Soros, *Journal of Economic Methodology*, 2013. Soros's later and shorter statement of the theory, free on his website.
- [Reflexivity, complexity, and the nature of social science](https://www.inet.ox.ac.uk/publications/reflexivity-complexity-and-the-nature-of-social-science), Eric Beinhocker, *Journal of Economic Methodology*, 2013. A reply in the same special issue that sharpens Soros's terms and argues that natural and artificial systems can be reflexive too.
