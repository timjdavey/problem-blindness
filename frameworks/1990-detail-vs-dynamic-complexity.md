# Detail vs dynamic complexity

In 1956 Jay Forrester moved from building computers to MIT's management school. He began modelling firms and cities as loops of stocks, flows and delays, which he called system dynamics. His 1971 paper "Counterintuitive behavior of social systems" argued that many American programmes to fix cities had made things worse. In 1990 his former student Peter Senge took these ideas to managers in The Fifth Discipline.

Senge split complexity into two kinds. Some problems have a huge number of parts, like a factory schedule with thousands of jobs. He called this detail complexity. Other problems may have only a few parts, but cause and effect are far apart. An action does one thing this month and the opposite next year. He called this dynamic complexity. The trait it sorts on is the gap between cause and effect. A problem can be huge in detail yet simple in its dynamics, or small and full of delays.

Senge argued that most forecasting and planning tools are built for detail. They add more rows to the spreadsheet, which does nothing for a delay. The person in charge sees a symptom, pushes the nearest lever and waits. Nothing seems to happen, so they push harder. Then the delayed effect lands on top of the extra push. Senge summed it up as "today's problems come from yesterday's solutions". The better response is to map the loops and delays before acting. Then make small moves and wait long enough to see what they did.

## Examples

**The beer game.** In the early 1960s Forrester's group at MIT built a board game about a supply chain. Four players run the links from shop to brewery. Each sees only the orders from the player below, and beer takes weeks to arrive. Customer demand rises once, from four cases a week to eight, and then holds steady. Orders still swing wildly up the chain, and the brewery ends up making far more beer than anyone wants. In 1989 John Sterman found the average team ran up costs about ten times the best possible. Players blame the customers or each other, but the cause is the delays in the chain they share.

**Putting out every fire.** After huge fires in 1910, the US Forest Service set out to stop all wildfires. In 1935 it made a rule that every fire should be under control by 10 a.m. the morning after it was reported. For decades this looked like success. But small fires had been clearing out dead wood, and without them the fuel piled up. The fires that escaped burned hotter. The agency dropped the rule in 1978, more than forty years after it began.
