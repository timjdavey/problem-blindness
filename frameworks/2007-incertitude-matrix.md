# Incertitude matrix

**Not knowing comes in four kinds, set by two questions. Do you know what could happen, and do you know how likely each outcome is?**

2007 · Andy Stirling · [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.embor.7400953) · Feeds 2 axes

## The idea

Andy Stirling studies science policy at the University of Sussex. Through the 1990s he watched British officials handle mad cow disease and GM crops, and kept seeing the same move. They asked experts for "the risk", got one number back and treated the matter as settled. In a 2007 paper in EMBO Reports, and again in a 2010 piece in Nature called "Keep it complex", he argued that the number often hid more than it showed.

He sorted not knowing with two questions. Do you know what outcomes are possible? And do you have good grounds for the odds of each one? If you know both, you face risk. If you know the outcomes but not the odds, you face uncertainty. If the odds may be known but people disagree about which outcomes count, you face ambiguity. If you don't even know what could happen, you face ignorance. Stirling put all four under one word, incertitude, so that risk becomes one corner of the map rather than the whole of it.

## What goes wrong

The mistake is to force all four into the risk corner, because only that corner gives a single answer. Uncertainty gets made up odds. Ambiguity gets one group's values built quietly into the sums. Ignorance gets left out, since you can't put a number on a thing nobody has thought of.

## What to do instead

The better response is to match the tool to the corner. Use ranges and scenarios for uncertainty, and open up the question of what counts for ambiguity. For ignorance, keep watching and prefer choices you can undo.

## Examples

### Mad cow disease

In February 1989 a UK working party led by Sir Richard Southwood reported that BSE was "most unlikely" to have any implications for human health. On 20 March 1996 the health secretary Stephen Dorrell told Parliament that ten people had a new form of CJD, most likely caught from BSE. The UK has since recorded 178 cases. The early advice treated ignorance as a small, known risk, when nobody yet knew how the disease could pass between species.

### CFCs and the ozone hole

In 1928 a team led by Thomas Midgley at General Motors created CFCs. They were prized for being stable and non-toxic, so they went into fridges and spray cans around the world. In 1974 Mario Molina and Sherwood Rowland warned that CFCs could thin the ozone layer slowly over decades, work that won them a share of the 1995 Nobel Prize. Then in May 1985 Joe Farman and his colleagues at the British Antarctic Survey reported a deep hole in the ozone over Antarctica. No model had predicted a loss that sudden in that place. A slow thinning was on the list of outcomes. A sudden hole over the South Pole was not, so no risk sum could have caught it.

### GM crops

In October 2003 the UK published the results of large farm trials of three GM crops bred to tolerate weedkiller. GM beet and oilseed rape left less food for wildlife than normal crops, while GM maize did better. Yet the public row was barely about these results. Farmers cared about yields, ecologists about birds and bees, and many others about who would own the seeds. More data on one outcome could not settle an argument about which outcomes matter.
