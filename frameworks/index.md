# Frameworks for defining problems

This lists every framework by date, oldest first. [Reading the frameworks](README.md) explains what they have in common and how they feed the axes. Where a date has a second year in brackets, the first is when the idea appeared and the second is the version most people cite.

# Frameworks, oldest first

* [Risk vs uncertainty](1921-risk-vs-uncertainty.md) - 1921, Frank Knight. Risk is when you know the odds, like a dice roll. Uncertainty is when you don't even know the odds, so you can't price it. Knight argued that profit comes from bearing the second kind.
* [Multi-armed bandit](1933-multi-armed-bandit.md) - 1933 (1952), William R. Thompson, Herbert Robbins. You choose again and again between options with noisy, unknown payoffs. Each choice pays out and also teaches you something. The problem is how to balance trying new options against using the best one so far.
* [Keynesian beauty contest](1936-keynesian-beauty-contest.md) - 1936, John Maynard Keynes. In some games you win by guessing what others will guess, not by judging the thing itself. Markets work like this, so the right answer moves whenever people change their minds about each other.
* [Unanticipated consequences](1936-unanticipated-consequences.md) - 1936, Robert K. Merton. Any action taken on purpose has results nobody meant, good or bad. Merton named five reasons people miss them, from plain ignorance to caring so much about the result they want that they never look past it.
* [Zero-sum vs non-zero-sum games](1944-zero-sum-vs-non-zero-sum-games.md) - 1944, John von Neumann, Oskar Morgenstern. In a zero-sum game one side's gain is the other's loss, so you face an opponent who adapts to you. In a non-zero-sum game both sides can win or lose together.
* [Use of knowledge in society](1945-use-of-knowledge-in-society.md) - 1945, Friedrich Hayek. Some answers need general knowledge that anyone can learn from a book. Others hang on facts about this place at this moment, which only the person on the spot has. Central planners, and AI models trained on general text, go wrong when they treat the second kind as the first.
* [Simplicity, disorganised complexity and organised complexity](1948-simplicity-disorganised-and-organised-complexity.md) - 1948, Warren Weaver. Problems of simplicity have a few variables, like a planet's orbit. Problems of disorganised complexity have millions of parts that average out, so statistics works. Problems of organised complexity sit between the two. They have many parts that interact, like an economy or a living cell.
* [Self-fulfilling prophecy](1948-self-fulfilling-prophecy.md) - 1948, Robert K. Merton. A false belief about a situation can make itself come true. A rumour that a bank will fail causes the run that makes it fail. The forecast changes the thing being forecast.
* [Portfolio selection](1952-portfolio-selection.md) - 1952, Harry Markowitz. The risk of a portfolio depends on how its parts move together, as well as on how risky each one is. Spreading your stake only cuts risk when the bets don't all fail at once. Bets that rise and fall together are one bet in disguise.
* [Clinical vs statistical prediction](1954-clinical-vs-statistical-prediction.md) - 1954, Paul Meehl. Meehl compared expert judgement with simple formulas across many prediction tasks. In noisy, repeated decisions the formulas matched or beat the experts almost every time. It marks out the problems where gut feel is the wrong tool.
* [Signal detection theory](1954-signal-detection-theory.md) - 1954, Wilson Tanner, John Swets. Any yes or no call can go wrong in two ways. You can raise a false alarm or you can miss the real thing. Where you set the bar should depend on what each mistake costs and how common the real thing is, not on how often you are right overall.
* [Small and large worlds](1954-small-and-large-worlds.md) - 1954, Leonard Savage. In a small world you can list every state of the world and every act before you choose, so you can look before you leap. In a large world some bridges can only be crossed when you reach them. Theories of rational choice work in the first kind and mislead in the second.
* [Johari window](1955-johari-window.md) - 1955, Joseph Luft, Harrington Ingham. A two by two grid of what you know about yourself against what others know about you. Its four boxes look a lot like the later known and unknown unknowns, though nobody has shown that one came from the other.
* [Convergent vs divergent thinking](1956-convergent-vs-divergent-thinking.md) - 1956 (1967), J. P. Guilford. Some problems have one right answer that the facts point to. Others have many good answers and reward range and fresh ideas. Mark a divergent task against a key, or brainstorm a question with one answer, and you get the wrong result.
* [Law of requisite variety](1956-law-of-requisite-variety.md) - 1956, W. Ross Ashby. A controller can only hold an outcome steady if it has at least as many different responses as there are disturbances to deal with. Only variety can absorb variety. So one tool, however good, can't cover a wide range of problem shapes.
* [Satisficing](1956-satisficing.md) - 1956, Herbert Simon. Real people and firms rarely search for the best answer. They set a bar for good enough and take the first option that clears it. Whether that is wise depends on how much the last bit of quality is worth.
* [Open vs closed skills](1957-open-vs-closed-skills.md) - 1957, E. C. Poulton. A closed skill is done in a world that holds still, like a golf drive, so you can plan the whole movement in advance. An open skill is done in a world that keeps moving, like returning a serve, so you have to watch and predict as you go. Each needs a different kind of practice.
* [Decision strategies matrix](1959-decision-strategies-matrix.md) - 1959, James D. Thompson, Arthur Tuden. Asks two questions. Do people agree on the goal, and do they agree on cause and effect? The four answers call for four strategies: computation, judgement, compromise and inspiration. It is the root of the Stacey matrix.
* [Programmed vs non-programmed decisions](1960-programmed-vs-non-programmed-decisions.md) - 1960, Herbert Simon. Programmed decisions come round often in the same form, so a fixed procedure can make them. Non-programmed decisions are new, messy and high stakes, so they need judgement. The mistake runs both ways: writing rules for one-off calls and treating everyday calls as special.
* [Ambiguity aversion](1961-ambiguity-aversion.md) - 1961, Daniel Ellsberg. People prefer a bet with known odds to one with unknown odds, even when the unknown bet is no worse. It shows that missing odds are a different kind of problem from bad odds.
* [Near decomposability](1962-near-decomposability.md) - 1962, Herbert Simon. Many complex systems are built from parts that interact strongly inside and weakly between. If a problem breaks up like this you can solve it one part at a time. If it doesn't, every fix touches everything else.
* [Sensitive dependence (chaos)](1963-sensitive-dependence-chaos.md) - 1963, Edward Lorenz. In some systems a tiny change at the start grows into a huge change later. Long range forecasts fail even when you know the rules perfectly. This is called the butterfly effect.
* [Well vs ill-structured problems](1964-well-vs-ill-structured-problems.md) - 1964 (1973), Walter Reitman, Herbert Simon. A well-structured problem has a clear goal, known moves and a way to check an answer, like chess. An ill-structured problem lacks one or more of these, like designing a house. Simon argued most real problems are ill-structured.
* [Clocks and clouds](1966-clocks-and-clouds.md) - 1966, Karl Popper. Some systems run like clocks and can be predicted far ahead. Others behave like clouds and can't. Most real systems sit somewhere between, and many we took for clocks are more cloudlike than they look.
* [Routine vs non-routine technology](1967-routine-vs-non-routine-technology.md) - 1967, Charles Perrow. Sorts work on two traits: how often odd cases turn up, and whether there is a logical way to work them out. That gives four kinds of work called routine, engineering, craft and non-routine. Each needs to be organised in a different way.
* [Matthew effect](1968-matthew-effect.md) - 1968, Robert K. Merton, Derek de Solla Price. Early success makes later success easier, so small or lucky leads grow into large gaps. Whoever is ahead gets more credit, more funding and more attention for the same work, and a winner's lead says less about their worth than it seems.
* [Convex vs non-convex optimisation](1970-convex-vs-non-convex-optimisation.md) - 1970 (2004), R. Tyrrell Rockafellar, then Stephen Boyd and Lieven Vandenberghe. In a convex problem the landscape has one valley, so going downhill always finds the best answer. A non-convex problem has many valleys. Where you start decides where you end up, and small changes can send you somewhere else.
* [Market for lemons](1970-market-for-lemons.md) - 1970, George Akerlof. When one side of a deal knows more than the other, the deals on offer are not a fair sample. Owners of good used cars won't sell at an average price, so the cars on sale are worse than average. The fact that something is offered to you is itself evidence about it.
* [NP problems](1971-np-problems.md) - 1971, Stephen Cook, Richard Karp, Leonid Levin. Some problems are hard to solve but easy to check, like a sudoku. Finding the answer may take ages. Once someone hands you an answer, you can test it fast.
* [Garbage can model](1972-garbage-can-model.md) - 1972, Michael Cohen, James March, Johan Olsen. In many organisations problems, solutions, people and chances to decide drift about on their own and get matched by timing more than by logic. Solutions go looking for problems they can be the answer to. It is the law of the instrument at the scale of a whole organisation.
* [Flat maximum](1973-flat-maximum.md) - 1973 (1982), Detlof von Winterfeldt, Ward Edwards. Near the best answer the payoff is often flat, so small errors in your weights or choices cost little. Where the payoff is peaked, the last bit of tuning matters a lot.
* [Resilience and stability](1973-resilience-and-stability.md) - 1973, C. S. Holling. Stability is how fast a system returns to normal after a push. Resilience is how big a push it can take before it flips into a different state. A forest ravaged by budworm every few decades is unstable yet has lasted for centuries, and holding a system steady can wear its resilience away.
* [Tame vs wicked problems](1973-tame-vs-wicked-problems.md) - 1973, Horst Rittel, Melvin Webber. A tame problem has a clear goal and a test of whether you solved it, like a chess puzzle. A wicked problem has neither. People disagree on what the problem even is and there is no point where it is done. Every attempt changes the situation, so you can't learn by trial and error. Rittel and Webber's ten traits bundle several separate ideas, which is why "wicked" feels strong but vague.
* [Messes](1974-messes.md) - 1974, Russell Ackoff. Real problems rarely come alone. Ackoff called a system of problems that act on each other a mess. Solving each part on its own can make the whole worse.
* [Goodhart's law](1975-goodharts-law.md) - 1975, Charles Goodhart. When a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. People game whatever you count, so the problem shifts in response to how you track it. Marilyn Strathern gave it its famous wording in 1997.
* [Agency costs](1976-agency-costs.md) - 1976, Michael Jensen, William Meckling. When one person decides and someone else pays for the mistakes, the person deciding takes risks they would never take with their own money. That gap is a real cost, and someone pays it in checks, in contracts or in losses.
* [Algorithm selection problem](1976-algorithm-selection-problem.md) - 1976, John R. Rice. When many methods could solve a problem, the best one often changes from case to case. If you can measure a few features of a case before you start, you can learn which method to pick for it. The method with the best average is often not the best choice for any one case.
* [Campbell's law](1976-campbells-law.md) - 1976, Donald T. Campbell. The more a number is used to make social decisions, the more it gets corrupted. It also warps the very thing it was meant to measure. It is the social science cousin of Goodhart's law.
* [Decisions with multiple objectives](1976-decisions-with-multiple-objectives.md) - 1976, Ralph Keeney, Howard Raiffa. Many problems have several goals that pull against each other. No single best answer exists until you say how much of one goal you would give up to get more of another.
* [Loosely coupled systems](1976-loosely-coupled-systems.md) - 1976, Karl Weick. In some organisations the parts respond to each other only weakly and slowly. That makes them hard to steer from the top. It also stops a failure in one part spreading to the rest.
* [Lucas critique](1976-lucas-critique.md) - 1976, Robert Lucas. Patterns in past economic data break once policy is built on them, because people change how they act when the rules change. A model of yesterday's behaviour fails on tomorrow's.
* [Presented vs discovered problems](1976-presented-vs-discovered-problems.md) - 1976, Jacob Getzels, Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi. Some problems are handed to you ready made. Others you have to find and frame yourself. In a study of art students, those who spent longest framing the problem made the most original work.
* [Single vs double-loop learning](1978-single-vs-double-loop-learning.md) - 1978, Chris Argyris, Donald Schön. Single-loop learning fixes errors against a fixed goal, like a thermostat. Double-loop learning questions the goal itself. Some problems can only be solved by the second kind.
* [Prospect theory](1979-prospect-theory.md) - 1979, Daniel Kahneman, Amos Tversky. People judge outcomes as gains or losses from where they stand now, not as final totals. Losses hurt more than gains of the same size please. So offered a sure gain, most people take it. Facing a sure loss, most will gamble to escape it, even when the gamble is worse on average.
* [Hard vs soft systems](1981-hard-vs-soft-systems.md) - 1981, Peter Checkland. In a hard problem people agree on the goal, so the job is to find the best way to reach it. In a soft problem people see the situation differently. The first job is to agree on what the problem is.
* [Skill, rule and knowledge-based behaviour](1983-skill-rule-and-knowledge-based-behaviour.md) - 1983, Jens Rasmussen. Familiar tasks run on trained skill. Known situations run on rules. New situations need slow reasoning from first principles. Many errors come from using the wrong level for the task.
* [High ground vs swampy lowlands](1983-high-ground-vs-swampy-lowlands.md) - 1983, Donald Schön. On the high ground problems are tidy and theory solves them. In the swamp they are messy and confusing, and they are often the ones that matter most. Professionals tend to stay on the high ground and solve the wrong problem well.
* [Normal accidents](1984-normal-accidents.md) - 1984, Charles Perrow. Sorts systems on two traits. Parts can interact in simple or tangled ways, and they can be loosely or tightly coupled. Tight coupling means there is no slack, so a failure spreads before anyone can stop it. When a system is both tangled and tight, accidents are built in.
* [Finite and infinite games](1986-finite-and-infinite-games.md) - 1986, James P. Carse. A finite game is played to win, so it has set players, set rules and an end. An infinite game is played to keep the play going, so it has no winner and no end. Treat an infinite game as finite and you declare victory, stop and lose what you held.
* [Funnel experiment](1986-funnel-experiment.md) - 1986, W. Edwards Deming. Drop marbles through a funnel at a target and move the funnel after each miss to cancel it, and the marbles spread wider, not narrower. Deming called this tampering. A reading that jumps about may be noise from a stable process, and correcting for noise adds to it.
* [Uncertainty vs equivocality](1986-uncertainty-vs-equivocality.md) - 1986, Richard Daft, Robert Lengel. Uncertainty is missing information, so you fix it by finding out more. Equivocality is a situation with several clashing meanings, so more data won't settle it. It needs people talking until they agree what is going on.
* [Cognitive continuum](1987-cognitive-continuum.md) - 1987, Kenneth R. Hammond, Robert Hamm, Janet Grassia, Tamra Pearson. Tasks sit on a line from ones that suit intuition to ones that suit analysis. People do best when the way they think matches where the task sits. Ill-structured tasks with many cues pull towards intuition, while tasks with clear rules pull towards analysis.
* [VUCA](1987-vuca.md) - 1987, US Army War College, from the work of Warren Bennis and Burt Nanus. Stands for volatility, uncertainty, complexity and ambiguity. It names four separate ways a situation can be hard, though people often use it as a single buzzword.
* [Reflexivity](1987-reflexivity.md) - 1987, George Soros. In markets, what people believe changes the facts, and the new facts then change what people believe. The loop means there is no fixed truth for a forecaster to find.
* [Rugged fitness landscapes](1987-rugged-fitness-landscapes.md) - 1987 (1993), Stuart Kauffman, Simon Levin. When the parts of a problem don't affect each other, small steps uphill always reach the top. When the parts interact, the landscape fills with small peaks. Step by step improvement then gets stuck on whichever peak is nearest.
* [Moravec's paradox](1988-moravecs-paradox.md) - 1988, Hans Moravec, with Marvin Minsky and Rodney Brooks. What is hard for people and what is hard for machines don't line up. Computers played draughts and passed intelligence tests long before they could see or pick things up, which a toddler does without trying. So "hard" means nothing until you say who is doing the solving.
* [Outcome bias](1988-outcome-bias.md) - 1988, Jonathan Baron, John Hershey. People judge a decision by how it turned out, not by what the person knew when they made it. A good call that ends badly gets blamed, and a reckless one that ends well gets praised. When even the best outcome on offer is a loss, whoever chose well is judged as if they chose badly.
* [Complex problem solving](1989-complex-problem-solving.md) - 1989, Dietrich Dörner. Dörner put people in charge of simulated towns and countries. Many linked variables, hidden states and time delays tripped up almost everyone. People acted on one thing and ignored the side effects.
* [Increasing returns](1989-increasing-returns.md) - 1989, W. Brian Arthur, with Paul A. David. When each new user makes a product more attractive to the next, a small early lead grows until one option takes the whole market. Which one wins can hang on chance events early on, and the winner need not be the best.
* [Detail vs dynamic complexity](1990-detail-vs-dynamic-complexity.md) - 1990, Peter Senge, from the work of Jay Forrester. Detail complexity means lots of variables. Dynamic complexity means cause and effect sit far apart in time and place, so the obvious fix often makes things worse. Most planning tools are built for the first and are blind to the second.
* [Individual vs group decisions](1990-individual-vs-group-decisions.md) - 1990, Donald Redelmeier, Amos Tversky. Doctors make different choices for one patient than for a group of patients just like them. Seen alone, a case feels special, so it gets the extra test and skips the awkward question. A choice about one case is often really a policy for many, and it should be judged that way.
* [Exploration vs exploitation](1991-exploration-vs-exploitation.md) - 1991, James G. March. Organisations must split effort between trying new things and refining what already works. Exploration pays off slowly and unreliably, so firms drift towards exploitation and get stuck.
* [Task characteristics of expertise](1992-task-characteristics-of-expertise.md) - 1992, James Shanteau. Experts do well in some fields, like weather forecasting and chess, and badly in others, like clinical psychology and stock picking. The difference lies in the task. Stable subjects, repeated decisions and fast feedback breed real skill.
* [Value-focused thinking](1992-value-focused-thinking.md) - 1992, Ralph Keeney. Most decisions start with a list of options and ask which is best. Value-focused thinking starts with what you want and uses it to design options nobody had put on the table. The best answer is often one you had to invent.
* [Post-normal science](1993-post-normal-science.md) - 1993, Silvio Funtowicz, Jerome Ravetz. Some questions have uncertain facts, disputed values, high stakes and urgent deadlines. Normal science can't settle them on its own. A wider group of people has to judge the quality of the answer, not only experts.
* [Inside vs outside view](1993-inside-vs-outside-view.md) - 1993, Daniel Kahneman, Dan Lovallo. You can judge a plan by its own details or by how similar plans turned out. The first view feels more informed but is usually too hopeful. Start from the track record of cases like yours, then adjust.
* [Technical vs adaptive challenges](1994-technical-vs-adaptive-challenges.md) - 1994, Ronald Heifetz. A technical problem can be solved with known expertise, like setting a broken leg. An adaptive challenge needs the people involved to change their values, habits or beliefs. No expert can solve it for them.
* [Task environment properties](1995-task-environment-properties.md) - 1995, Stuart Russell, Peter Norvig. Sorts the world an agent acts in by a few plain questions. Can it see everything, does chance play a part and does the world change while it thinks? Later editions added whether anyone else is acting. A crossword is easy on every count and driving a taxi is hard on every count.
* [Stacey matrix](1996-stacey-matrix.md) - 1996, Ralph Stacey. Plots a decision by how much people agree on it and how sure they are of cause and effect. Near agreement and certainty, you can plan and control. Far from both you are in chaos. The zone between is where complexity methods help.
* [Four levels of uncertainty](1997-four-levels-of-uncertainty.md) - 1997, Hugh Courtney, Jane Kirkland, Patrick Viguerie. A strategy faces one of four levels. There may be a clear enough future, a few distinct futures, a range of futures, or true ambiguity. Each level needs different tools, and firms go wrong by treating every case as the first.
* [No free lunch](1997-no-free-lunch.md) - 1997, David Wolpert, William Macready. Averaged over every possible problem, all search methods do exactly as well as each other. A method only wins by matching the shape of the problem in front of it. There is no best tool in general.
* [Taboo trade-offs](1997-taboo-trade-offs.md) - 1997 (2000), Alan Fiske, Philip Tetlock. People trade time against money every day without a second thought. Trading money against a child's life feels wrong even to consider, because it treats a sacred value as something with a price. Methods that turn every value into one score break on these problems.
* [Accountability](1999-accountability.md) - 1999, Jennifer Lerner, Philip Tetlock. Having to explain a choice to someone else changes how you make it. Sometimes it makes you more careful. Often it just makes you pick what is easiest to defend.
* [Cynefin](1999-cynefin.md) - 1999 (2003), Dave Snowden, Cynthia Kurtz. Splits situations into clear, complicated, complex and chaotic, plus confusion when you don't know which you are in. In clear and complicated problems cause and effect can be worked out in advance. Complex ones only make sense looking back, and chaotic ones make no sense at all. Each needs a different way to act.
* [Ecological rationality](1999-ecological-rationality.md) - 1999, Gerd Gigerenzer, Peter Todd, ABC Research Group. A rule of thumb is not good or bad in itself, only good or bad for a given setting. Simple rules can beat complex models when data is scarce and the odds are uncertain. They lose when data is plentiful and the world holds still.
* [Problem typology](2000-problem-typology.md) - 2000, David Jonassen. Lists eleven kinds of problem, from logic puzzles to design problems and dilemmas. They differ in how structured, how complex and how tied to their setting they are. Each kind needs to be taught and solved in a different way.
* [Simple, complex and wicked](2000-simple-complex-and-wicked.md) - 2000, Nancy Roberts. Sorts problems by whether people agree on the problem and on the fix. Simple problems have agreement on both. Complex ones agree on the problem but not the fix. Wicked ones agree on neither, and each kind needs a different way of coping.
* [Ladder of causation](2000-ladder-of-causation.md) - 2000 (2018), Judea Pearl, later with Dana Mackenzie. Questions about cause sit on three rungs: seeing, doing and imagining. Data from a lower rung can't answer a question from a higher one on its own. You need a test, or a stated model of what causes what.
* [Critical transitions](2001-critical-transitions.md) - 2001 (2009), Marten Scheffer and colleagues. Many systems have more than one stable state. A slow push can do nothing visible for years and then tip a clear lake into a murky one in a season, and undoing the push doesn't tip it back. Near the edge a system recovers more slowly from small knocks, which is one of the few warnings you get.
* [Kind vs wicked learning environments](2001-kind-vs-wicked-learning-environments.md) - 2001 (2015), Robin Hogarth, later with Tomás Lejarraga and Emre Soyer. In a kind environment feedback is quick, accurate and matches the real task, so experience teaches you well. In a wicked environment feedback is missing, late or misleading. Experience there can teach you the wrong lesson.
* [Puzzles vs mysteries](2001-puzzles-vs-mysteries.md) - 2001, Gregory Treverton. A puzzle has an answer that more information would reveal, like where a missile is hidden. A mystery has no single answer even with all the facts, like what a leader will do next. You can only frame a mystery well, not solve it.
* [Known and unknown unknowns](2002-known-and-unknown-unknowns.md) - 2002, Donald Rumsfeld. Known knowns are things we know. Known unknowns are gaps we know about. Unknown unknowns are gaps we don't even know to look for, and they cause the worst surprises. The idea is older, but Rumsfeld made it famous.
* [Simple, complicated and complex](2002-simple-complicated-and-complex.md) - 2002, Sholom Glouberman, Brenda Zimmerman. Following a recipe is simple. Sending a rocket to the moon is complicated, but once solved it can be repeated. Raising a child is complex, because each child is different and success with one doesn't guarantee the next.
* [Deep uncertainty](2003-deep-uncertainty.md) - 2003, Robert Lempert, Steven Popper, Steven Bankes. Parties can't agree on how the system works, how likely each outcome is, or how to value the outcomes. Instead of predicting, you look for plans that hold up across many possible futures. This grew into decision making under deep uncertainty (DMDU).
* [Levels of uncertainty](2003-levels-of-uncertainty.md) - 2003 (2013), Warren Walker and colleagues, later with Robert Lempert and Jan Kwakkel. Grades uncertainty from a clear future, through known odds and plausible scenarios, up to total ignorance. It also asks where the uncertainty sits, such as in the inputs or in the model itself.
* [Constrained vs unconstrained skills](2005-constrained-vs-unconstrained-skills.md) - 2005, Scott G. Paris. Some skills are a small, closed set that nearly everyone masters, like the alphabet. Others have no ceiling, like vocabulary. Scores on the first kind only track the second while children are still learning, so drilling them gives gains that fade.
* [Tame, wicked and critical](2005-tame-wicked-and-critical.md) - 2005, Keith Grint. Tame problems call for management. Wicked problems call for leadership that asks good questions. Critical problems are crises with no time to talk, so they call for command. Grint argues leaders often pick the label that justifies the style they prefer.
* [Mediocristan vs Extremistan](2007-mediocristan-vs-extremistan.md) - 2007, Nassim Nicholas Taleb. In Mediocristan no single case moves the total much, like human height. In Extremistan one case can outweigh all the rest, like wealth or book sales. Averages and past data work in the first and mislead in the second.
* [Super wicked problems](2007-super-wicked-problems.md) - 2007 (2012), Kelly Levin, Benjamin Cashore, Graeme Auld, Steven Bernstein. Wicked problems with four extra traits. Time is running out, and the people trying to solve it are also causing it. There is no central authority, and policies discount the future. Climate change is the standard example.
* [Incertitude matrix](2007-incertitude-matrix.md) - 2007, Andy Stirling. Not knowing comes in four kinds, set by two questions. Do you know what could happen, and do you know how likely each outcome is? Only when you know both is it risk, and only then does a single number make sense.
* [Fourth quadrant](2008-fourth-quadrant.md) - 2008, Nassim Nicholas Taleb. A grid of how complex the payoff is against how wild the odds are. Simple bets in tame worlds are safe to model. Complex payoffs in fat-tailed worlds form the fourth quadrant, where statistics break down and errors cost the most.
* [Conditions for intuitive expertise](2009-conditions-for-intuitive-expertise.md) - 2009, Daniel Kahneman, Gary Klein. Intuition can be trusted only when two things hold. The setting must be regular enough to learn, and the expert must have had long practice with quick, clear feedback. Without both, confident experts are often wrong.
* [Fragile, robust and antifragile](2012-fragile-robust-and-antifragile.md) - 2012, Nassim Nicholas Taleb. Sorts things by how they respond to shocks. Fragile things break, robust things stay the same and antifragile things get better. Resilient things, which Taleb lumps in with robust, bend and then recover. What matters is the shape of your exposure, not the accuracy of your forecast.
* [One-way vs two-way doors](2015-one-way-vs-two-way-doors.md) - 2015, Jeff Bezos. A one-way door decision can't be undone, so it deserves slow and careful thought. A two-way door can be reversed cheaply, so you should decide fast and learn from the result.
* [Ergodicity](2016-ergodicity.md) - 2016 (2019), Ole Peters, Murray Gell-Mann. Some bets look good on average across a crowd yet ruin almost everyone who keeps playing them. The test is whether the average over many people at one moment matches the average for one person over time. When it doesn't, expected value is the wrong guide.
* [Need for interpretability](2017-need-for-interpretability.md) - 2017, Finale Doshi-Velez, Been Kim. We only need a model to explain itself when the problem can't be fully written down. If the goal is complete and well tested, a score is enough. When parts of the goal are missing, like fairness, safety or the law, an explanation lets a person check what the score leaves out.
* [Radical uncertainty](2020-radical-uncertainty.md) - 2020, John Kay, Mervyn King. Many real problems can't be given probabilities at all, because we can't list what might happen. Kay and King argue you should ask "what is going on here?" and build a story, rather than pretend to calculate odds.
* [Cohesion](2023-cohesion.md) - 2023, Tim J. Davey. When runs of a system disagree, they can still fall into a few clear groups, like snowflakes that are all different yet each a plate, a column or a star. Clear groups let you plan for each one and spot early which you are in. Runs with no groups give you nothing to latch onto.
