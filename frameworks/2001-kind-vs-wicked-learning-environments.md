# Kind vs wicked learning environments

We trust experience. Someone who has done a job for twenty years should be better at it than someone who started last week. Robin Hogarth, a psychologist, pointed out that this only holds if the job gives you honest feedback. In his 2001 book *Educating Intuition* he split the places we learn into two kinds. The trait this sorts on is whether the feedback you get is quick and honest.

In a kind environment you act and then quickly find out whether you were right. The feedback is accurate, and the practice looks like the real thing. Over time your gut gets good. In a wicked environment the feedback is missing, slow or wrong. You may never see the result of your choice, or you may see a result that your choice itself caused. In 2015 Hogarth wrote it up again with Tomás Lejarraga and Emre Soyer, and David Epstein's book *Range* later made the idea popular.

The danger is that experience in a wicked environment still feels like expertise. You grow more confident with each year, and you have a long list of times you were right. But the lessons can be false. Treat a wicked environment as kind and you end up trusting a gut that was trained on bad data. The better response is to ask how an expert's gut was trained. Where the feedback was poor, lean on records, base rates and outside checks instead.

Wicked is a bundle, not one trait. In the 2015 paper the three authors split learning into two settings: the one where you learn and the one where you use what you learnt. An environment is kind when the two match and wicked when they don't, and they can fail to match in several ways. Each way has its own axis here:

- The grade comes late or never ([Immediate vs delayed feedback](../axes/immediate-vs-delayed-feedback.md)).
- The grade is noisy, so luck passes for skill ([Deterministic vs statistical](../axes/deterministic-vs-statistical.md)).
- You only see how your own choices turned out, never the road not taken ([Interventional vs observational](../axes/interventional-vs-observational.md)).
- Your own action causes the result you take as proof ([Inert vs reflexive](../axes/inert-vs-reflexive.md)).
- Practice doesn't look like the real task ([Stationary vs drifting](../axes/stationary-vs-drifting.md)).

This framework feeds the [Deterministic vs statistical](../axes/deterministic-vs-statistical.md), [Immediate vs delayed feedback](../axes/immediate-vs-delayed-feedback.md), [Inert vs reflexive](../axes/inert-vs-reflexive.md), [Stationary vs drifting](../axes/stationary-vs-drifting.md) and [Interventional vs observational](../axes/interventional-vs-observational.md) axes.

## Examples

**The typhoid doctor.** Hogarth tells a story first written down by the physician Lewis Thomas. An early 20th century New York doctor was famous for spotting typhoid before any symptoms showed. He felt each patient's tongue with his bare hands, and his guesses kept coming true. They came true because he was carrying typhoid from patient to patient. The feedback told him he was a brilliant judge when he was the cause.

**Darts.** Each dart lands a second after you throw it, and you can see exactly how far off it was. You throw again and adjust. This is about as kind as learning gets, which is why a few months of practice makes such a big difference.

**Hiring.** A manager who has hired for years trusts their instinct about candidates. But they only ever see how the people they hired turned out. They never learn how the ones they turned away would have done. The feedback is half missing, so the instinct can stay wrong for a whole career.
