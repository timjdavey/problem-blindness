# Uncertainty vs equivocality

In 1986 Richard Daft and Robert Lengel wrote a paper on how firms handle information. Most theory then treated a manager's problem as missing information, cured by more data. Daft and Lengel argued there was a second problem. They drew on the psychologist Karl Weick, who had argued that the main reason people organise at all is to agree what confusing events mean.

The first problem is a lack of information. Think of twenty questions, where each yes or no answer narrows things down until you name the object. This is called uncertainty. The second problem is a situation that can be read in several ways that clash. Here you can't ask a yes or no question, because you don't know what the question is. This is called equivocality. They paired this with a ranking of channels by how well they carry meaning, which they called media richness. Talking face to face is richest, because you get instant feedback and tone of voice. Tables of numbers are the leanest. Rich channels suit equivocal problems, and lean ones suit uncertain problems.

The mistake is to treat an equivocal problem as an uncertain one. You commission another study or send a report, and each side reads the data as proof of its own view. Daft and Lengel said managers reduce equivocality by defining or creating an answer, rather than by finding one in more data. The better response is to get the people who see it differently into a room. Let them argue it out until they agree a shared reading. Then any data you gather has a clear question.

## Examples

**Khrushchev's two letters.** In October 1962 the US found Soviet nuclear missiles in Cuba. On 26 October a long and personal letter from Nikita Khrushchev offered to remove the missiles if the US promised not to invade Cuba. The next day a harsher letter on Moscow radio added a demand that the US remove its own missiles from Turkey. No spy photo could say which letter showed what Moscow wanted. Kennedy's advisers argued it out face to face, and they chose to answer the first letter as the real offer. Robert Kennedy privately told the Soviet ambassador that the missiles in Turkey would go. On 28 October Khrushchev agreed to withdraw.

**Columbia's foam strike.** On 16 January 2003, about 82 seconds after launch, foam from the fuel tank hit the left wing of the space shuttle Columbia. Engineers argued over what it meant, and their findings reached managers as slides. One slide was headed "Review of Test Data Indicates Conservatism for Tile Penetration". Near the bottom it noted that the test pieces were far smaller, and the foam that hit Columbia was about 640 times bigger. The engineers' doubt was the real message, and the slide buried it. The problem was not missing data. The engineers had test results but disagreed on what they meant for a piece of foam this big, and a lean channel like a slide could not carry that argument. On 1 February Columbia broke up on its way home, killing all seven crew. The investigation board criticised NASA's "endemic use of PowerPoint briefing slides instead of technical papers".
