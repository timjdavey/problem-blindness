# Clocks and clouds

**Some systems run like clocks and can be predicted far ahead. Others behave like clouds and can't.**

1966 · Karl Popper · [Key book](https://archive.org/details/objectiveknowled00popp) · Feeds 2 axes

## The idea

On 21 April 1965 the philosopher Karl Popper gave a lecture at Washington University in St Louis in memory of the physicist Arthur Holly Compton. It was published in 1966 as "Of Clouds and Clocks" and later collected in his book *Objective Knowledge* in 1972. Popper wanted to explain how people could have free will in a world run by physics. To do it he drew a line with clouds at one end and clocks at the other.

Clocks stand for systems that are regular and orderly, so you can predict them far ahead. The solar system is the best example. Clouds stand for systems that are irregular and disorderly, so you can't. His example was a swarm of gnats, where each insect darts about at random but the swarm hangs together. The trait this sorts on is where a system sits on that line. Popper's story was about how physics got this wrong. After Newton, many scientists believed that every cloud was really a clock they didn't understand yet. This idea that the world might in principle be fully predictable is called determinism, and Popper argued it was false. In the 20th century the picture flipped. Quantum physics suggested that even the best clock has some cloud in it.

## What goes wrong

The mistake is to treat clouds as if they were clocks. Clocks reward careful models and precise forecasts, so an expert trained on clocks keeps building them. Point that skill at an economy, an election or a war and it produces confident numbers that miss.

## What to do instead

The better response is to ask first how cloudlike the system is. For clocks, predict and plan. For clouds, give ranges rather than single numbers, watch closely and keep plans easy to change.

## Examples

### The 1715 eclipse

Edmond Halley used Newton's laws to map where a total eclipse of the sun would cross England on 3 May 1715 (22 April in the calendar England then used). He got the timing right to within about four minutes and the path to within about 20 miles. Astronomers can now give the time of an eclipse centuries ahead. Over human timescales the solar system really is a clock.

### Political science

In 1977 Gabriel Almond and Stephen Genco wrote "Clouds, Clocks, and the Study of Politics" in the journal *World Politics*. They argued their field had copied physics and treated politics as a clock, though politics is made of people who learn and change their minds. The psychologist Philip Tetlock later put numbers on the problem. From 1984 to 2003 he collected 82,361 forecasts from 284 experts on politics and economics. Most did little better than chance, and the famous ones tended to do worse.
