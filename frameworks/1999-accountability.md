# Accountability

We tend to assume that making people explain their choices makes those choices better. Philip Tetlock spent the 1980s testing that idea in the lab. In 1999 he and Jennifer Lerner pulled together the research in a review in Psychological Bulletin called "Accounting for the effects of accountability". Their answer was that it helps far less often than people think.

Accountability means you expect to have to justify your choice to someone. Lerner and Tetlock found it only makes people think harder under narrow conditions. You have to know you will be judged before you make up your mind. The audience has to care about how you reasoned, not only about the result. Most of all, you mustn't know what answer the audience wants. When you do know, you tend to give it to them. When you have already decided, you tend to spend your effort defending that choice instead of testing it.

The trait this sorts on is whether a choice has to be explained and defended. That changes which methods you can use. A gut call that works may still be unusable if it can't be put into words. A simple rule that you can point to is easier to defend, even when it does worse. The mistake is to treat explaining as free, or to assume it always improves the choice. The better response is to ask who will judge the choice, what they want and whether the method you plan to use can be shown to them.

## Examples

**Turning down a loan.** The US Equal Credit Opportunity Act requires lenders to tell an applicant the main reasons they were refused credit. A lender can't just say a model gave a low score. So many lenders use credit scores built from a small set of factors they can name, like late payments or high balances. A more complex model might predict defaults better, but it is harder to turn into a list of reasons.

**Defending a choice you already made.** In a 1989 study Tetlock, Linda Skitka and Richard Boettger asked people for their views on contested policies. Those who expected to explain themselves to an audience whose views they didn't know thought through both sides more. Those who had already taken a public stand mostly spent their effort building a case for it. The same pressure made one group more careful and the other more stubborn.
