# Campbell's law

**The more a number is used to make social decisions, the more it gets corrupted. It also warps the very thing it was meant to measure.**

1976 · Donald T. Campbell · [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.56645/jmde.v7i15.297) · Feeds 2 axes

## The idea

Governments like numbers because they seem fair. A test score or a crime rate looks the same whoever reads it, so it feels safer to fund schools or judge police chiefs by it. Donald Campbell, a social psychologist who spent his career on how to test public programmes, saw the catch. The more weight a number carries in decisions about people, the more those people push on it. Some of them fiddle the figures. Others change what they do so the figures look good, even when the real work gets worse.

That second harm is what sets Campbell apart from [Goodhart](1975-goodharts-law.md). Goodhart said the measure stops telling you the truth. Campbell said the measure also bends the activity it was meant to watch. Teachers drill the test instead of teaching the subject. Police record crimes in the way that scores best, rather than the way that helps victims.

## What goes wrong

The framework sorts problems by how much is riding on a number and whether the people it judges can move it. A number used only for research can stay honest. A number that decides pay or funding or jobs won't for long. The mistake is to treat a social measure as a neutral reading when it has become a target. You then reward the gaming and punish the people who refuse to take part.

## What to do instead

Campbell's own advice was to use several measures and to keep some judgement that no number can replace.

## Examples

### Atlanta's schools

In the 2000s Atlanta's public schools posted gains in test scores that won national praise. Bonuses and jobs depended on hitting score targets. In 2011 a state inquiry found cheating in 44 schools, with 178 teachers and principals named. Staff had changed pupils' answers after the tests. In 2015 eleven educators were convicted of racketeering. The scores had gone up while many children were still behind, and the real gaps were hidden for years.

### The NYPD tapes

In 2010 the Village Voice published secret recordings made by Adrian Schoolcraft, a New York police officer in a Brooklyn precinct. On them, bosses pushed officers to hit their numbers and to record some crimes as less serious than they were. Falling crime figures made the precinct look good to headquarters. The tapes suggested the figures were partly a product of the pressure to produce them.

### Teaching to the test

Anyone who sat exams at school has seen a milder version. Once a school is ranked on one exam, lessons drift towards past papers and away from the parts of the subject the exam doesn't cover. The grades rise, but the pupils don't know the subject any better.
