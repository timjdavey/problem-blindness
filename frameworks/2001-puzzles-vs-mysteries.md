# Puzzles vs mysteries

**A puzzle has an answer that more information would reveal. A mystery has no single answer even with all the facts.**

2001 · Gregory Treverton · [Original book](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511754470) · Feeds 1 axis

## The idea

Some questions have a definite answer that is simply hidden from you. Find the missing piece and you are done. Other questions have no fixed answer yet, because the answer depends on choices people haven't made. Gregory Treverton, who worked in American intelligence, called the first kind puzzles and the second kind mysteries. He set this out in his 2001 book *Reshaping National Intelligence for an Age of Information*. Malcolm Gladwell made the idea popular in a 2007 essay in *The New Yorker*.

The trait that splits them is whether more facts would settle it. During the Cold War, how many warheads a Soviet missile could carry was a puzzle. The number existed, and spies and satellites could find it. A puzzle suffers from too little information. A mystery is different. More facts help, but they never close the question, and too much information can bury the signal you need.

## What goes wrong

The mistake is treating a mystery as a puzzle. Agencies and firms respond to a mystery by collecting ever more data, as if the answer were sitting in a file somewhere.

## What to do instead

The better response is judgement: weigh the facts you have and think about what the people involved want.

Gladwell's version drifted from Treverton's. For Treverton a mystery has no answer yet, because it hangs on choices still to be made. Gladwell also used the word for a fact that already exists but is buried in too much information, which is the Enron case below. That second kind does have an answer, so it is really a puzzle that is hard to read. It is worth asking which one someone means when they say mystery.

## Examples

### Finding bin Laden

Where Osama bin Laden was hiding after 2001 was a puzzle. He was in one place and the right piece of information would reveal it. In 2011 American intelligence traced a courier to a compound in Abbottabad, Pakistan, and the puzzle was solved.

### Enron

Gladwell argued that Enron was a mystery, in his wider sense, not a puzzle. Much of what was wrong with the firm was there in its public filings. The trouble was making sense of them, not finding a hidden secret. Enron filed for bankruptcy in December 2001.

### Iraq after the invasion

Whether Iraq still held weapons of mass destruction in 2003 was a puzzle, and in the end there was a factual answer. What would happen in Iraq once Saddam Hussein was gone was a mystery. No amount of spying could reveal it, because it depended on millions of choices not yet made.
