# Base rates

**How useful a test is depends on how common the thing it looks for is. When the thing is rare, most of the people a good test flags don't have it.**

1955 · Paul Meehl and Albert Rosen · [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0048070) · Feeds 1 axis

## The idea

In the early 1950s clinical psychologists were hunting for signs on personality tests that would pick out, say, the patients likely to attempt suicide or the ones with brain damage. A sign was judged by how well it sorted a sample of known cases. In 1955 Paul Meehl and Albert Rosen pointed out in *Psychological Bulletin* that this left out the number that mattered most. They called it the base rate, the share of people in the group being tested who have the condition at all.

A sign is built on a sample where the condition is common, often half the cases. In the clinic the condition may be one case in a hundred. The sign still catches most of the real cases, but it also flags a few per cent of the healthy, and there are a hundred times more of them. So most of the people it flags are healthy. Meehl and Rosen worked out when a sign beats the dull rule of betting on the base rate and saying no to everyone. For a rare condition it has to be very accurate indeed, and a sign that looked good in the lab can make more mistakes in the clinic than no sign at all. The trait this sorts on is how common the thing you are looking for is among the cases you see.

In 1973 Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed that people don't just skip this step when the numbers are hard. They skip it even when told. Given a description of a man and told he was drawn from a pool of 70 engineers and 30 lawyers, people judged his job from the description alone, and gave the same answer when the pool was 30 engineers and 70 lawyers. Maya Bar-Hillel named this the base rate fallacy in 1980. In 1995 Gerd Gigerenzer and Ulrich Hoffrage found a fix. Put the same problem as counts, like one person in a thousand and fifty false alarms in a thousand, and far more people get it right than when it is put as percentages.

## What goes wrong

The mistake is to read a test's accuracy as the chance that a positive is real. In 1978 Ward Casscells and his colleagues asked 60 staff and students at Harvard Medical School about a disease that hits one person in a thousand and a test that gives a false alarm for 5% of healthy people. What is the chance that someone who tests positive has the disease? The right answer is about 2%. Eleven gave it. Twenty-seven said 95%.

## What to do instead

The better response is to start from how common the thing is, not from how good the test is. Count it out. In a thousand people, one has the disease and the test catches them. Fifty of the other 999 get a false alarm. A positive is one real case among about fifty-one, so the chance it is real is about 2%. Then check that the test beats betting on the base rate at all, and if it can't, use it only to pick who gets a second, better test.

## Examples

### The Harvard doctors

Casscells's question had a rate of one in a thousand and a false alarm rate of 5%. In a thousand people, one tests positive and is ill, and about fifty test positive and are well. Most of the Harvard group read the 5% false alarm rate as a 95% chance of disease. Their answer was off by a factor of about fifty, and these were people who read test results for a living.

### Engineers and lawyers

Kahneman and Tversky gave people short sketches of men drawn from a pool and asked how likely each was to be an engineer. One group was told the pool held 70 engineers and 30 lawyers, the other that it held 30 engineers and 70 lawyers. The sketch should have counted for more in the first group than the second. It counted the same in both. A sketch that said nothing either way got a judgement near 50% in both groups, when it should have given 70% in one and 30% in the other.
