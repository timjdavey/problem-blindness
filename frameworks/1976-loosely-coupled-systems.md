# Loosely coupled systems

A head teacher brings in a new policy in September, and by Christmas most classrooms look much as before. Each teacher shuts the door and teaches the way they know. In 1976 Karl Weick argued in *Administrative Science Quarterly* that this isn't a fault to be fixed. It is how many organisations are built. His example was the school counsellor's office. It is attached to the principal's office, but each keeps its own identity and they affect each other rarely, weakly or slowly. He called this a loosely coupled system.

The trait this sorts on is how strongly the parts respond to each other. Charles Perrow later used the same idea in *Normal Accidents* (1984) to explain why tight systems fail all at once. Weick came first and looked at the upside. When one part breaks down, a loose system seals the damage off. Each part can adapt to local conditions, and the system keeps a stock of different ways of working for when the world changes. The cost is that loose systems are hard to steer. Orders from the top fade on the way down, and a good idea in one classroom doesn't spread to the next.

The mistake runs both ways. One is to treat a loose system as if it were tight. You change the policy at the top and assume it flows through to the front line. The other is to tighten the links for control or efficiency and lose the buffering that kept one failure from spreading. The better response is to work out how tightly the parts are linked before you act. In a loose system, change has to be built in each part, and the slack is worth keeping.

## Examples

**Mrs O's revolution.** In 1985 California published a new mathematics framework that told teachers to move away from drills and rote learning towards understanding. David Cohen studied a teacher he called Mrs Oublier and published the case in 1990. She believed her teaching had gone through a revolution. Cohen saw that she had taken on the visible features, like objects for children to handle and small groups. Yet underneath she still taught much as before. The policy and the look of the classroom had changed, but the teaching barely moved.

**The Aisin fire.** Toyota runs on just-in-time, so it keeps very little stock. In 1997 about 99% of its brake proportioning valves came from one Aisin Seiki factory in Kariya. That link was tight, with no buffer. When the factory burned on 1 February 1997, Toyota had to shut its assembly plants in Japan. The rescue came from the looser side of the Toyota group. Other suppliers worked out their own ways to make the valve, and even a sewing machine maker was persuaded to help. Assembly restarted on 5 February.
