# Time inconsistency

**A trade between now and later gets decided again each time now comes round, and now always wins. Settle it while later is still far off, then bind it.**

1955 · Robert Strotz · [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.2307/2295722) · Feeds 1 axis

## The idea

Economists had long assumed that a person planning how to spend over a lifetime would make a plan and keep it. In 1955 Robert Strotz showed in the *Review of Economic Studies* that this only holds for one special way of weighing the future. If you weigh next week against next year in the same proportion as you weigh this week against next week, the plan you make today still looks right tomorrow. If, as most people do, you weigh the very near future far more heavily than anything beyond it, then a trade you settle today in favour of later will look wrong once later becomes now. Strotz called this inconsistency, and the sliding scale behind it myopia.

He saw two ways out. One is precommitment. Ulysses wanted to hear the Sirens and knew he would steer for the rocks once he did, so he had himself bound to the mast and his crew's ears stopped with wax. The other is consistent planning. Make only the plans your future self will actually keep, and give up on the rest. The trait this sorts on is whether the costs and the benefits of a choice fall at the same time. When they don't, the person who decides and the person who pays are the same person at different ages, and they disagree.

Later work filled in the shape of the curve and what to do about it. In 1975 George Ainslie found that pigeons and people both prefer a smaller reward now to a larger one later, then switch to the larger one when both are moved into the future. He called it hyperbolic discounting. Jon Elster's 1979 book *Ulysses and the Sirens* made self-binding a theory of rationality. Richard Thaler and Hersh Shefrin modelled each person as a planner and a doer in 1981, and David Laibson in 1997 showed how a saver who knows their own weakness will lock money away where they can't reach it.

## What goes wrong

The mistake is to assume today's trade will stand. Every January the gym fills and every February it empties. Every budget cuts spending next year rather than this one. Each time the later date arrives it has become now, and now gets the heavier weight. The trade is decided afresh, and it goes the same way every time. A plan that counts on a future self with different weights from today's is a plan that will be broken, and it is broken by someone who meant it when they made it.

## What to do instead

The better response is to settle the trade while later is still far enough off that it gets a fair weight, then make the decision hard to reverse. Thaler and Shlomo Benartzi did this with pension saving in 2004. Workers were asked, months in advance, to commit to raising their saving rate at each future pay rise, so the cost never showed up as a cut in take home pay. Where no binding is possible, Strotz's second route stands. Plan only what your future self will keep.

## Examples

### Ulysses

Ulysses's trade was between hearing the Sirens' song and living. Settled from a distance it is easy. Settled within earshot, every sailor who tried had chosen the song. He didn't trust his future self and he was right not to. The wax and the rope let him keep the choice he made while his weights were sane.

### Save More Tomorrow

At the first firm to try Thaler and Benartzi's plan, most of the workers offered it joined. Their saving rate went from 3.5% of pay to 13.6% over 40 months. Nothing about the trade had changed. What changed was when it was decided, and that undoing it took an effort.
