# Fourth quadrant

**A grid of how complex the payoff is against how wild the odds are. Complex payoffs in fat-tailed worlds form the fourth quadrant, where statistics break down.**

2008 · Nassim Nicholas Taleb · [Original essay](https://www.edge.org/conversation/nassim_nicholas_taleb-the-fourth-quadrant-a-map-of-the-limits-of-statistics) · Feeds 2 axes

## The idea

Some questions only have two answers. Will it rain tomorrow? Will this patient survive the year? Other questions are about size. How much rain will fall? How much will the bank lose? Taleb's point was that statistics copes well with the first kind even in a wild world. It copes badly with the second kind once the world gets wild.

He drew a grid with two traits. The first is the payoff. It can be simple, like a yes or no. Or it can be complex, where the size of the outcome matters and has no upper limit. The second trait is the world the odds come from. It can be tame, where no single case can move the total much. Or it can be fat-tailed, where one huge event can outweigh everything else. The first three boxes are fairly safe to model. The fourth, with complex payoffs in a fat-tailed world, is where models fail and failure costs the most.

## What goes wrong

The error is to treat a fourth quadrant problem as if it sat in one of the other three, because the maths looks the same on paper.

## What to do instead

Taleb's advice for the fourth quadrant is not a better model. It is to cut your exposure to the events the model can't see. That means less debt and more slack, and avoiding bets where a rare loss could wipe you out.

## Examples

### Banks in 2007

Banks summed up their risk as a single number for how much they might lose on a bad day. This is called value at risk, and it assumed that losses roughly followed a bell curve. In August 2007 David Viniar of Goldman Sachs said the firm was seeing "25-standard deviation moves, several days in a row". Under a bell curve a single move that size should never happen. A trading book has complex payoffs in a fat-tailed market, which is the fourth quadrant.

### A pandemic

Whether you catch flu this winter is a yes or no question, and doctors can put fair odds on it. How many people a new virus will kill is a question of size in a fat-tailed world. Most outbreaks fade. A few kill tens of millions, as the 1918 flu did. Planning for that from the average of past outbreaks puts you in the fourth quadrant.

### A kitchen refit

Whether your builder turns up on Monday is a simple question. How much the job will cost in the end is not. A rotten joist or a hidden pipe can double the bill, so the right move is to keep a large buffer rather than trust the quote.
