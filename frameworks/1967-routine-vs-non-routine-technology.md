# Routine vs non-routine technology

**Sorts work on two traits: how often odd cases turn up, and whether there is a logical way to work them out.**

1967 · Charles Perrow · [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.2307/2091811) · Feeds 3 axes

## The idea

In the 1960s sociologists compared organisations by size or goals. Charles Perrow thought this missed the point. In a 1967 paper in the *American Sociological Review* he argued you should compare them by the work they do on their raw material. He called that work their technology. By this measure, he noted, some schools are like prisons and some factories are like universities.

He sorted work on two traits. The first is how often a case turns up that doesn't fit, which Perrow called an exception. The second is what happens when one does. Sometimes there is a logical way to search for the answer, as when an engineer works out an odd load. Sometimes there isn't, and people fall back on hunch and guesswork. Perrow called these problems analysable and unanalysable. The two traits make four cells. Routine work has few exceptions and clear methods, like a steel mill or a factory making screws and bolts. Engineering has many exceptions but clear methods, like a firm building heavy machinery. Craft has few exceptions but no clear method, like making specialty glass. Non-routine work has many exceptions and no clear method, like aerospace.

Each cell suits a different shape of organisation. Routine work runs well on rules, central control and little freedom for the people doing it. Non-routine work needs skilled staff who have the power to decide, and who talk to each other often.

## What goes wrong

The common mistake is to run craft or non-routine work as if it were routine. Managers write procedures and set targets, then force the cases that don't fit through the wrong process.

## What to do instead

The better response is to ask how often surprises arrive and whether anyone can reason them out. Then set the rules and the freedom to match.

## Examples

### Ford's Model T

In 1913 Ford's moving assembly line at Highland Park cut the time to build a chassis from about 12.5 hours to about 1.5. It worked because Ford made one model in one way, for years in only one colour. When buyers wanted variety, that strength became a weakness. Ford built its 15 millionth Model T in May 1927, then shut its plants for about six months to retool. Some 60,000 workers were laid off, and Chevrolet passed Ford in sales that year.

### Mental hospitals

Perrow used the same grid for work on people. A custodial mental hospital treats its patients as alike and well understood, so its work is routine. A hospital that sets out to cure its patients finds each one different and poorly understood, which is the non-routine cell. Perrow noted that some hospitals saw their methods fell short of their goals and tried to move from one cell to the other. That takes more than a new therapy, because rules built for custody stop ward staff using their judgement.
