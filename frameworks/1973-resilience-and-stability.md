# Resilience and stability

Every few decades the spruce budworm explodes across the fir forests of eastern Canada and kills huge stands of trees. By the usual measure of the time the forest was unstable, since it never settled at one level and swung wildly. Yet it had lasted for centuries. C. S. Holling, a Canadian ecologist, asked how a system could be so unsteady and so lasting at once.

His answer was that two different things had been sharing one word. The first he kept calling stability. It is how quickly and smoothly a system returns to where it was after a push. The second he called resilience. It is how big a push the system can take and still keep working the same way, before it flips into a different state altogether. The budworm forest scores low on the first and high on the second. A lake that stays perfectly clear until one bad year turns it murky for good scores high on the first and low on the second. Holling later called the two engineering resilience and ecological resilience.

The split matters because the two call for different management. If you only measure stability you will try to hold the system steady, by cutting every swing and running it at its best level. Holling argued that this can wear resilience away without anyone noticing, so a system that looks well run is closer to the edge than it seems. He said managers should keep their options open and assume that future events will be unexpected. A resilience approach doesn't need you to forecast the next shock, only to build something that can absorb it.

## Examples

**Spruce budworm.** Holling's main worked example was a model of the budworm and the fir forests it eats. The model has two stable states, one with few budworm and one with an outbreak. It flips between them when the forest grows old and dense enough, and it doesn't flip back at the same point. The swings are part of how the forest keeps going.

**Lake Michigan trout.** From 1898 to 1940 the lake trout catch in Lake Michigan held at around six million pounds a year. By the 1950s the trout were close to extinct there. The trigger was the sea lamprey, which had spread through the Great Lakes after the Welland Canal was built. Holling noted that the lamprey were still very few when the trout collapsed. A small extra loss was enough, because decades of fishing at the limit had already used up the stock's ability to take a blow. The fishery had looked stable the whole time.
