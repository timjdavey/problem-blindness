# Survivorship bias

**The cases you can study are the ones that made it back. A record of survivors tells you what survives, not what happened.**

1943 · Abraham Wald · [Original memo](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA091073.pdf) · Feeds 2 axes

## The idea

Abraham Wald was a Hungarian mathematician who fled to America in 1938 and spent the war in the Statistical Research Group at Columbia, working on problems the military sent over. One was where to put armour on bombers. Armour is heavy, so it has to go where a hit brings the plane down. The data was the damage on planes that came back from missions. In 1943 Wald wrote a series of memos on how to read it.

His point was that the planes in the data were the ones that had survived. A hit that brought a plane down was never counted, because that plane never came back. So the parts of a returning plane that showed few hits were not the parts the enemy missed. They were the parts where a hit was fatal. Wald built a method to estimate, from the pattern of hits on survivors, how likely a hit on each part was to bring a plane down. Marc Mangel and Francisco Samaniego set the method out in 1984, and the Center for Naval Analyses reprinted the memos in 1980 because the problem kept coming round. The trait this sorts on is whether the cases you have were filtered by the outcome you want to study. Bill Casselman has shown that the popular telling, in which Wald told the Navy to armour where the holes weren't, rests on thin records. The method is Wald's. The anecdote may not be.

The same filter runs through any record that only keeps what lasted. Funds that close drop out of the databases, so the funds left look better than the average investor did. Edwin Elton, Martin Gruber and Christopher Blake estimated in 1996 that this overstated American mutual fund returns by about 0.9% a year. Stephen Brown and his colleagues had shown in 1992 that the filter alone can make fund performance look persistent when it isn't. Advice on how to succeed comes from the people who did, which is why it rarely mentions the things that sank the rest.

## What goes wrong

The mistake is to learn from the cases that came back as if they stood for all cases. The armour goes where the holes are, which is where a plane can take a hit. The investor picks the fund with the longest good record, which is partly the fund that was lucky enough to last. The founder copies the habits of the firms that made it, which the firms that failed shared.

## What to do instead

The better response is to ask, before reading any pattern, how each case got into the record and what would have kept it out. Then ask what the missing cases would look like. Where you can, go and find them. Where you can't, treat the record as a sample of the survivors and reason about the rest, as Wald did.

## Examples

### The bombers

Returning bombers showed hits across the wings and fuselage and few on the engines. Read straight, that says to armour the wings. Read with the lost planes in mind, it says a plane hit in the wings can fly home and a plane hit in the engine can't. Wald's method assumed hits were spread across the plane in the air, and used the gaps in the survivors' damage to work out where the losses had been hit.

### Mutual funds

A fund database that drops closed funds keeps only the ones that did well enough to stay open. Elton, Gruber and Blake compared the records with and without the closed funds and found the gap was about 0.9% a year across the industry, and bigger for small funds. A fund choosing its peer group can go further. Leave out the closed funds and a large share of the survivors can claim a top quarter record.
