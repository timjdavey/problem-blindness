# Decisions with multiple objectives

When you buy a house you want a low price, a short commute and a good school nearby. The cheap house is far out and the one near the good school costs more, so no house wins on everything. There is no best house until you say how many minutes of commute you would swap for how many pounds. This framework sorts problems on whether there is one thing to get right or several that pull against each other.

Decision analysis used to pick one number to maximise, usually money. In 1976 Ralph Keeney and Howard Raiffa published *Decisions with Multiple Objectives: Preferences and Value Tradeoffs*, which showed how to handle several goals at once. You list the objectives and pick a measure for each one. Then you ask the person deciding how much of one they would give up to get more of another. These are called value trade-offs, and the maths that joins them into one choice is called multiattribute utility theory.

The mistake comes in two forms. One is to chase a single goal and ignore the rest, like buying the cheapest house and then spending two hours a day on a train. The other is to fold all the goals into one score too early, so the trade-offs get made inside a formula nobody looks at. The better response is to name each objective and measure it on its own terms. Then make the trade-offs out loud, where people can see them and argue.

This framework feeds the [Single objective vs trade-offs](../axes/single-objective-vs-trade-offs.md) axis.

## Examples

**Mexico City airport.** In the summer of 1971 Mexico's Ministry of Public Works asked Richard de Neufville, Keeney and Raiffa to help plan its airports. One option was to expand the existing airport by Lake Texcoco, which the city had grown around. The other was a new airport at Zumpango, about 40 kilometres to the north. Keeney scored each plan on six objectives. The first three were cost, safety and capacity. The rest were noise, social disruption and travel time to the airport. A site near the city keeps trips short but puts more homes under the flight path. Zumpango was picked in 1974 but never built. A new airport at Texcoco was cancelled after a public vote in 2018, and one at Zumpango opened in March 2022.

**Nuclear sites in Washington.** The Washington Public Power Supply System wanted sites for new nuclear plants in Washington and parts of Oregon and Idaho. Keeney and K. Nair cut the map down to nine candidate sites. They scored each one on six objectives covering health and safety, harm to the environment, effects on local people and money. Rather than crown one winner, they tested how the ranking moved as the weights changed. Their advice, published in 1977, was to study three of the sites in detail before picking one.
