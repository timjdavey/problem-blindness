# One-way vs two-way doors

In his 2015 letter to Amazon's shareholders, Jeff Bezos split decisions into two kinds. Some are like a door you can only walk through once. If you don't like what's on the other side, you can't go back. Others are like a door you can walk back through if the room turns out to be wrong. He called these one-way and two-way doors, or Type 1 and Type 2 decisions, and the door names are the ones that stuck.

The trait that sorts them is how cheaply you can undo the choice. A one-way door deserves slow and careful thought with a wide range of views, because a mistake is permanent. A two-way door deserves the opposite. You should decide fast, often with one person or a small team, and learn from what happens. Bezos argued that most decisions are two-way doors.

He warned that large firms tend to use the heavy one-way process for every decision. The result is slowness and fear of risk, and too little trying of new things. The opposite mistake is rarer but worse. Treating a one-way door as if it were two-way means rushing through something you can't take back. Getting the label right first saves time on the small calls and care for the big ones.

## Examples

**New Coke.** In April 1985 Coca-Cola replaced its nearly century old formula with a sweeter one. The public backlash was fierce. Within three months the company brought the old drink back as Coca-Cola Classic. Because the firm had kept the original recipe, a decision that looked like a one-way door turned out to be two-way.

**Germany's nuclear plants.** After the Fukushima disaster in 2011, Germany decided to speed up closing all its nuclear power stations. The last three shut in April 2023. Once a reactor is shut down and taken apart, starting it again is slow and costly, and often impossible. You can change your mind about the policy later, but you can't easily change your mind about the plants.

**Painting a room.** Painting a room a colour you end up hating costs a weekend and a few tins of paint. Knocking down a wall costs far more to put back. It makes sense to try the paint quickly and save your worry for the wall.
