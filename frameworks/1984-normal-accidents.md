# Normal accidents

**Systems differ on two traits. Parts can interact in simple or tangled ways, and they can be loosely or tightly coupled.**

1984 · Charles Perrow · [Original book](https://books.google.com/books/about/Normal_Accidents.html?id=N3hRAAAAMAAJ) · Feeds 3 axes

## The idea

Charles Perrow, an American sociologist, asked why some systems keep having disasters even when everyone does their job. His answer sorts systems on two traits. The first is how the parts interact. In a linear system one step follows another in an order you can see, like an assembly line. In a complex system parts affect each other in ways the designers didn't plan and the operators can't see. The second trait is coupling. A loosely coupled system has slack, so a fault can wait while someone sorts it out. A tightly coupled system has none, so one failure sets off the next within minutes.

Put the two together and you get a grid. A university is complex but loose, so odd things happen and there is time to fix them. A dam is tight but linear, so failures are fast but easy to follow. When a system is both complex and tight, small faults combine in ways nobody expected and spread before anyone understands them. Perrow called the result a normal accident. He didn't mean it was common. He meant it was a normal property of that kind of system.

## What goes wrong

The mistake is to treat these systems as ones where accidents come from bad parts or bad people. Then the fix is more safety devices and more rules. Perrow argued those can add new links and new ways to fail, which makes the system more complex still.

## What to do instead

Sometimes the honest answer is to add slack, or to decide the system isn't worth running.

## Examples

### Three Mile Island

Perrow began the book after studying the 1979 accident in Pennsylvania. A failed pump, a stuck valve and a misleading light came together. The light showed that the valve had been told to close, not that it had closed. Each fault was small and none was unusual on its own. Together they let coolant drain from the reactor for over two hours before the operators understood what was going on.

### The flash crash

On 6 May 2010 US share indices were already down more than 4% for the day. Then they fell a further 5 to 6% in minutes and climbed back almost as fast. Automated traders react to each other in fractions of a second, which is tight coupling. The links between their strategies are hidden, which is complexity. Regulators responded with circuit breakers that pause trading in any share that moves 10% in five minutes. That adds slack, which is the kind of fix Perrow favoured.
