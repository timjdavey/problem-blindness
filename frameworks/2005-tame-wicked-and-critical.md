# Tame, wicked and critical

**Tame problems call for management, wicked problems for leadership and critical problems for command.**

2005 · Keith Grint · [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018726705061314) · Feeds 3 axes

## The idea

Keith Grint took Rittel and Webber's split between tame and wicked problems and added a third kind. A tame problem may be hard but it has been solved before, like heart surgery. It calls for management, which means applying the right process. A wicked problem has no clear answer and no end point, like crime or the future of the NHS. It calls for leadership, which for Grint means asking good questions and getting people to own the problem. A critical problem is a crisis with no time to talk, like a fire in a building. It calls for command, where one person tells everyone what to do. The trait this sorts on is whether a problem has a known answer, no known answer or no time to look for one.

Each style rests on a different kind of power. Command uses force or the threat of it. Management uses pay and process. Leadership has to rely on people choosing to follow, because nobody can order a wicked problem away. So the three styles are not grades of the same skill. They are different tools for different kinds of problem.

## What goes wrong

Grint's sharpest point is that these labels are chosen, not found. A leader who likes giving orders has every reason to call a problem a crisis. A manager who likes process will call a wicked problem tame and launch another target. The label then justifies the style, and the problem gets the wrong treatment.

## What to do instead

When someone tells you what kind of problem you face, ask what that label lets them do.

## Examples

### The Grenfell Tower fire

The first call about the fire at Grenfell Tower in west London came at 12.54am on 14 June 2017. Residents were told to stay in their flats, which was the standard advice for tower blocks. That rule is a tame answer to a normal fire, where walls stop flames spreading between flats. Here the cladding let the fire race up the outside of the building, so the problem had become critical. The stay put advice was not dropped until 2.47am, and 72 people died. The public inquiry found it should have been dropped much earlier.

### The road to war in Iraq

In September 2002 the British government published a dossier claiming Iraq could deploy chemical or biological weapons within 45 minutes of an order. Framing the problem as an urgent threat turned a wicked question into a critical one. Critical problems call for command and leave little room for debate. The long and messy years after the invasion showed the problem had been wicked all along.

### The urgent project at work

Most people have had a boss who calls every project urgent. By framing each task as a crisis, they skip the slow work of asking others what the problem really is. It feels decisive, but the hard questions never get asked.
