# Task characteristics of expertise

By the early 1990s research on experts gave a confusing picture. Some studies found experts far better than novices. Others found them barely better than chance. James Shanteau, an American psychologist who studied expert judgement, looked across these studies and argued the difference lay in the task, not the people. Some fields let experts become truly skilled, and others don't, however clever the people in them are.

He listed experts on each side. Weather forecasters, chess masters, test pilots and accountants tended to do well. Clinical psychologists, parole officers, court judges and stockbrokers tended to do poorly. What split the lists was the shape of the work. Good performance came where the thing being judged was stable and decisions came often in a similar form. It also needed feedback that was quick and clear. It helped when the problem broke into parts and when aids like tables or formulas were common. Poor performance came where the subject was human behaviour, feedback was rare or late, and every case felt one of a kind.

So the trait this sorts on is whether the task lets experience turn into skill. The danger is that experts in the poor fields feel just as sure as those in the good ones. Confidence and years of practice look the same from outside, so we trust a parole officer's gut as we would trust a pilot's.

## Examples

**Weather forecasters.** A forecaster says there is a 70% chance of rain and finds out the next day whether it rained. They make thousands of these calls a year, all in the same form. Studies of US forecasters found their stated odds matched how often it actually rained very closely. That is rare skill, and the task is what built it.

**Parole decisions.** A parole officer judges whether a prisoner will reoffend. The subject is a person who will keep changing. Feedback comes years later if at all, and never for prisoners who were kept inside. Experience in this job can't teach much, however long it lasts.

**Stock picking.** Fund managers study firms all day, yet most active funds trail a simple index fund over ten years or more. Prices move with news nobody can foresee. The feedback is so noisy that luck and skill look alike for years.
