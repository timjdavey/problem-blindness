# Tame vs wicked problems

A chess puzzle tells you exactly what counts as solved: mate in three. You can try a line, see it fail and try another, and nobody is hurt. Many public problems are nothing like this. Ask ten people what the problem with a city's housing is and you get ten answers. Every answer points to a different fix, and every fix changes the city for good. Horst Rittel and Melvin Webber called the first kind tame and the second kind wicked.

They wrote in 1973, after a decade in which planners had hoped the methods that put men on the moon could also fix poverty and crime. Their paper lists ten traits of a wicked problem. There is no agreed statement of the problem and no point where it is finished. Answers are better or worse, never right or wrong. Each attempt is a one-shot operation, because it leaves marks that can't be undone. Each problem is also unique and is often a symptom of another one.

The mistake is to treat a wicked problem as tame. You pick one definition and set a target, then build the fix as if it could be tested and rolled back. When it fails, the situation has moved and the people affected have paid. The label has a weakness too. The ten traits mix up separate ideas, such as disagreement over goals, the lack of a stopping point and the lack of trial and error. That is why "wicked" feels strong but is hard to act on. The better response is to ask which of those traits your problem really has, and to deal with each one on its own terms.

## Examples

**Boston's Central Artery.** In 1959 Boston finished an elevated highway through the middle of the city. Planners treated traffic as a tame problem: build a road big enough to carry it. The road cut the North End off from the rest of downtown and was soon jammed. The fix was to bury it. The Big Dig ran from 1991 to 2007 and cost about $14.6 billion. Every attempt at a wicked problem leaves marks, and these took half a century and billions to undo. Pruitt-Igoe, the St Louis housing estate, is told under [High ground vs swampy lowlands](1983-high-ground-vs-swampy-lowlands.md).

**What causes crime?** Rittel and Webber used crime to show that the way you explain a wicked problem decides the fix. If crime comes from poverty, you fund jobs. If it comes from weak policing, you hire officers. If it comes from drugs, you change drug laws. None of these explanations can be proven wrong in a way that settles the argument, so the choice of story is itself part of the problem.
