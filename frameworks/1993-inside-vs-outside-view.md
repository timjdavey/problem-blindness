# Inside vs outside view

In the 1970s Daniel Kahneman was writing a school course on judgement for Israel's ministry of education. A year in, he asked his small team to guess how long the rest would take. Their answers ran from 18 to 30 months. Then he asked Seymour Fox, the team's curriculum expert, how long similar teams had taken. Fox went quiet. About 40% never finished, he said, and he couldn't recall any that took less than seven years or more than ten. The team carried on anyway. It took eight years, and by then the ministry had lost interest and the course was never used. In 1993 Kahneman and Dan Lovallo used this story in a paper on why managers forecast so badly.

The trait here is how you look at a case. You can treat it as unique and build a forecast from its own plans, people and steps. Or you can treat it as one of a crowd of similar cases and ask how that crowd turned out. Kahneman and Lovallo called these the inside view and the outside view. The crowd is called a reference class, and its track record is the base rate. The inside view feels richer because you know the details. But the details you know are the ones going to plan. The delays, the illness and the change of minister come from outside the plan, so the inside view never sees them.

The mistake is trusting the inside view because it feels better informed. Fox had the base rate in his head, yet his own guess had been as hopeful as everyone else's. The better response is to start outside. Find a fair group of past cases and use their spread of results as your first guess. Then nudge it up or down for what really is different about yours. Bent Flyvbjerg later turned this into a method for big public projects, called reference class forecasting.

## Examples

**Edinburgh trams.** In 2004 the UK Department for Transport adopted Flyvbjerg's method, and its first use was on Edinburgh's tram plans. The promoters had added 25% to their base cost for overruns. A reference class of past rail projects said 57% was needed to be 80% sure of staying in budget. The scheme went ahead and still ran far over. Plans costed at £375 million in 2003 ended up as one shorter line for £776 million, opened in 2014.

**Public works.** In 2002 Flyvbjerg and colleagues looked at 258 transport projects in 20 countries. Almost nine in ten cost more than forecast, and rail projects ran about 45% over on average.
