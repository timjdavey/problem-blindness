# Ecological rationality

**A rule of thumb is not good or bad in itself, only good or bad for a given setting.**

1999 · Gerd Gigerenzer, Peter Todd and the ABC Research Group · [Original book](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL388028M) · Feeds 4 axes

## The idea

Most of us assume more data and more sums give better answers, so a shortcut is a lazy second best. In 1999 Gerd Gigerenzer, Peter Todd and the ABC Research Group in Berlin argued otherwise in *Simple Heuristics That Make Us Smart*. A rule of thumb is only good or bad for a given setting, the way a key only fits its own lock. They called this fit ecological rationality. The trait it sorts problems on is the world the rule meets: how much data there is, how noisy it is and how steady.

Their boldest claim is that ignoring information can make you more accurate. A complex model with many dials can fit the past closely, but much of what it fits is noise. When data is thin, luck sets the dials and the forecasts swing about. A simple rule can't bend to fit the noise, so it is wrong in a steady way and often less wrong overall. Statisticians call this the bias-variance dilemma. In 2009 Gigerenzer and Henry Brighton argued that the smaller the sample and the less predictable the outcome, the more the simple rule pays. When data is plentiful and the world stays put, the careful model wins.

## What goes wrong

The mistake is to bring tools built for long records and known odds to a problem with little data and unknown odds. The reverse mistake is to trust a rule of thumb in a stable, data rich world where a proper model would do better.

## Examples

### Take-the-best

In the book Jean Czerlinski, Gigerenzer and Daniel Goldstein ran a contest across 20 real data sets on topics from car accidents on a stretch of highway to how attractive people rated public figures. Each task was to pick the higher of two things. Take-the-best checks one clue at a time, best clue first, and stops at the first one that tells the two apart. Multiple regression weighs every clue. On data it had already seen, regression came first at 77% correct. When each method learnt from half the data and was tested on the other half, regression lost the most and take-the-best won.

### San Diego or San Antonio

In 2002 Goldstein and Gigerenzer described asking about a dozen Americans and Germans which city is larger. About two thirds of the Americans got it right. Every German did, though about half had never heard of San Antonio and just picked the city they knew. That only works where fame tracks size, as it does with cities.

### The 1/N portfolio

In 2009 Victor DeMiguel, Lorenzo Garlappi and Raman Uppal tested 14 optimising models against splitting money equally across N assets. None was consistently better than 1/N. By their sums, the classic method would need about 3,000 months of data to beat it with 25 assets. That is 250 years, far longer than any market's record.
