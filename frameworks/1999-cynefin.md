# Cynefin

Some problems have a known answer anyone can look up. Some have an answer only an expert can work out. Some have no answer you can know in advance, and you only see what worked once it has worked. And some are moving so fast that there is no pattern at all. Dave Snowden began building this idea at IBM in 1999, and the version most people cite is his 2003 paper with Cynthia Kurtz. He named it Cynefin, a Welsh word for the place you belong.

The trait it sorts on is how cause and effect relate. In a clear situation they are obvious, so you follow best practice. In a complicated one they can be found by analysis, so you call in an expert. In a complex situation causes only make sense looking back, so you run small safe experiments and grow what works. In a chaotic one there is no time to find causes, so you act first to make things stable. A fifth state, confusion, is when you don't know which of the four you are in. The 2003 paper called these known, knowable, complex, chaos and disorder. Snowden has renamed them since, and this page uses the current names.

Snowden's warning is that people pick the domain they are used to, not the one they are in. Experts see every problem as complicated and reach for analysis. Managers want everything to be clear and reach for process. Treat a complex problem as complicated and you write a perfect plan for a world that won't stand still. The better response is to work out which domain you are in before you pick a method, and to expect parts of one problem to sit in different domains.

## Examples

**The Ferrari and the rainforest.** Snowden liked to say a Ferrari is complicated and a rainforest is complex. A good mechanic can take a Ferrari apart and put it back together, and it will run the same. You can't do that with a rainforest, because its parts keep changing each other.

**The children's party.** Snowden's favourite complex example is a party for a group of young children. You don't write a minute by minute plan. You set some limits, put out a few things to do and watch. When a game starts to go wrong you step in, and when something works you let it run.

**11 September 2001.** In the first hours after the attacks on New York and Washington, nobody had time to analyse what was going on. The job was to act: close the airspace, clear the buildings and stop things getting worse. Only once things were stable could people step back and start to make sense of it.
