# Convergent vs divergent thinking

**Some problems have one right answer that the facts point to. Others have many good answers and reward range and fresh ideas.**

1956 · J. P. Guilford · [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0040755) · Feeds 1 axis

## The idea

In 1950 J. P. Guilford gave his presidential address to the American Psychological Association, and he used it to complain. He had searched about 121,000 titles in Psychological Abstracts and found only 186 that dealt with creativity. Guilford worked at the University of Southern California, where he used statistics to pull apart the separate abilities that make up intelligence. In 1956 he set out his map in a paper called "The structure of intellect" in Psychological Bulletin. In it he split the abilities that produce answers into two kinds, convergent thinking and divergent thinking. By his 1967 book *The Nature of Human Intelligence* he called them convergent and divergent production, two of the five operations in a model of 120 abilities.

Convergent thinking is for problems where the facts point to one best answer, like a sum or a crossword clue. Divergent thinking is for problems with many good answers, where range and novelty count, like listing uses for a brick. The trait this sorts on is whether a problem has one right answer or many good ones. This is not the same as whether a problem is well structured. "Write a sonnet about autumn" is well structured, since it has a fixed form, a clear topic and an obvious end. Yet there are endless good sonnets about autumn. Nor is it the same as creative against dull, because a clever puzzle can still have exactly one answer.

## What goes wrong

The mistake runs both ways. Mark divergent work against one key and you punish the good answer nobody listed, or you fall back on whatever is easy to count. Brainstorm a problem with one right answer and you get a pile of ideas when you needed the answer, and no vote will pick it out.

## What to do instead

The better response is to ask how many answers would count as right before you choose a method. If there is one, check against it. If there are many, judge each answer on its merits and expect good answers you didn't foresee.

## Examples

### The SAT essay

In 2005 the SAT added a required essay. Les Perelman, who ran undergraduate writing at MIT, studied the graded sample essays the College Board had published. He found that length alone predicted the score more than 90% of the time. One essay that got a perfect six said the Civil War began with "the firing of two shots at Fort Sumter in late 1862". The bombardment began in April 1861 and lasted more than a day. The scorers' guide said writers "may make errors in facts or information that do not affect the quality of their essays". Perelman's point was that an essay has no answer key, so the marking drifted to something easy to count. Meanwhile the one part with right answers went unmarked. The College Board made the essay optional in 2016 and dropped it after June 2021.

### The Remote Associates Test

In 1962 Sarnoff Mednick argued in Psychological Review that creativity means joining ideas that sit far apart. With Martha Mednick he built a test to measure it. Each item gives three words and asks for a fourth word that links them all. For cottage, Swiss and cake the answer is cheese. The adult form has 30 items and a 40 minute limit. It was built to measure creativity, yet every item has exactly one right answer. Many researchers now treat it as a test of convergent thinking. What decides the kind of task is how many answers count as right, not whether it feels creative.

The axis draws the line a little differently. Many good answers that differ only in degree, like phones that trade battery life for size, count as convergent there, because a blend of two is still good. Only answers that differ in kind count as divergent.
