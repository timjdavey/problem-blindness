# Messes

**Real problems rarely come alone. Ackoff called a system of problems that act on each other a mess.**

1974 · Russell Ackoff · [Key paper](https://doi.org/10.1057/jors.1979.22) · Feeds 2 axes

## The idea

Most training teaches you to take a big problem and cut it into small ones. You solve each piece, put them back together and expect the whole to be solved. That works when the pieces don't affect each other. Russell Ackoff argued that in real organisations and cities they nearly always do. He called a set of problems that act on each other a mess.

The trait this framework sorts on is how tightly problems are linked. In a loose set you can fix one thing and the rest stay put. In a mess, fixing one part shifts the others. A gain in one place can turn up as a loss somewhere you weren't looking. Ackoff, one of the pioneers of operational research in America, thought his own field had become too good at solving the parts and too bad at seeing the whole.

## What goes wrong

The mistake is to treat a mess as a list of separate problems. Each team hits its target and the overall picture gets worse. The fix for one department becomes the next department's problem.

## What to do instead

Ackoff's answer was to design the whole system on purpose, starting from what you want it to be, rather than patching each fault as it appears.

## Examples

### The Aral Sea

In the 1960s Soviet planners set out to grow far more cotton in Central Asia. They diverted the two rivers that fed the Aral Sea, then one of the four largest lakes in the world, to irrigate the fields. The cotton targets were met. But the sea shrank to a small fraction of its old size, the fishing industry collapsed and ports like Muynak were left stranded far from the water. Winds now carry salt and farm chemicals from the dry seabed across the region. Treated alone, the cotton problem was solved. Treated as a mess, the region was far worse off.

### The Katy Freeway

Houston widened the Katy Freeway to as many as 26 lanes, finishing in 2008 at a cost of about $2.8 billion. The goal was to cut jams. Within a few years the jams were back. From 2011 to 2014 peak journey times on parts of the road rose by 30 to 55%. New lanes drew new drivers and new homes further out, so the traffic problem was tied up with housing and where people chose to live.

### A household budget

A family short of money cuts back on childcare to save cash. One parent now works fewer hours, so income drops and the budget gets tighter. Each choice made sense alone. Together they made the whole worse.

## Further reading

- [The future of operational research is past](https://doi.org/10.1057/jors.1979.22), Russell Ackoff, *Journal of the Operational Research Society*, 1979. Ackoff's attack on his own field, where he says managers face messes, not separate problems. The idea first appeared in his 1974 book *Redesigning the Future*.
- [Social mess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_mess) on Wikipedia, a section of the article on wicked problems that quotes Ackoff's definition.
- [The Aral Sea disaster](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.earth.35.031306.140120), Philip Micklin, *Annual Review of Earth and Planetary Sciences*, 2007. How irrigation drained the sea and what followed.
- [The fundamental law of road congestion: evidence from US cities](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.101.6.2616), Gilles Duranton and Matthew Turner, *American Economic Review*, 2011. Evidence that new roads fill with new traffic, as on the Katy Freeway.
