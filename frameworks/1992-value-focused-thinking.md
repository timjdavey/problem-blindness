# Value-focused thinking

**Value-focused thinking starts with what you want and uses it to design options nobody had put on the table.**

1992 · Ralph Keeney · [Original book](https://archive.org/details/valuefocusedthin0000keen) · Feeds 1 axis

## The idea

In 1960 Herbert Simon split decision making into three phases in *The New Science of Management Decision*, the same book that gave us [programmed and non-programmed decisions](1960-programmed-vs-non-programmed-decisions.md). Intelligence is noticing that a decision is needed. Design is inventing and working up the possible courses of action. Choice is picking one of them. Most decision analysis works on the last phase, comparing options that someone has already drawn up. Ralph Keeney, who had co-written [Decisions with Multiple Objectives](1976-decisions-with-multiple-objectives.md), argued this was backwards. In 1992 Harvard University Press published his book *Value-Focused Thinking: A Path to Creative Decisionmaking*.

Keeney called the usual habit alternative-focused thinking. A problem turns up, you list the options in front of you and only then ask what you want, so that you can rank them. He calls this reactive. Value-focused thinking flips the order. First you set out what you truly care about in this decision. Then you use that to create new options, and to spot decisions worth making before anyone forces them on you. The trait this sorts on is whether the options are handed to you or whether you have to design them. This is different from how big the search is. A huge search still assumes the options already exist and the job is to look through them. Here the best option may not exist until you make it.

## What goes wrong

The mistake is to take the menu as given. You pick the least bad of what is on the table and never notice you could have built something else.

## What to do instead

The better response is to write down what you want, and why, before you compare anything. For each thing you want, ask which option would do best on that alone. Then try to build one that does well on several at once. Keep the old way for choices where the options truly are fixed, like a ballot paper.

## Examples

### New York's water

The Catskill and Delaware reservoirs supply New York City with an average of 1.3 billion gallons of water a day for about nine million people. A federal rule from 1989 meant the city would have to filter that water unless it could keep the source clean. The city estimated a filtration plant would cost $6 billion to build and more than $300 million a year to run. Rather than start by pricing plants, the city went back to what it wanted, which was clean water at the tap. On 21 January 1997 it signed an agreement with the towns in the watershed, New York State, the federal Environmental Protection Agency and environmental groups. The deal cost about $1.5 billion over ten years and included more than $250 million to buy land around the reservoirs from willing sellers. In December 2017 the state gave the city a further ten year waiver from filtering.

### Room for the River

In 1993 and 1995 the Rhine and the Meuse flooded. In 1995 the dikes only just held, and about 250,000 people in the Netherlands had to leave their homes. The option on the table was the old one of building the dikes higher. But each raise puts more water behind the dike, so a breach does more harm. The Dutch government started from two goals instead. The branches of the Rhine had to carry 16,000 cubic metres of water a second by 2015, and the land along the rivers had to get better, not worse. From 2006 it moved dikes back, lowered floodplains and dug side channels at more than 30 places. At Nijmegen a dike was moved 350 metres inland between 2013 and 2015, and a 3 kilometre channel was dug in the space. The island this made is now a city park. The work cost €351 million, and the channel first carried flood water in February 2016.
