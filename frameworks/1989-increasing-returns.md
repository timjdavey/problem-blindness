# Increasing returns

**When each new user makes a product more attractive to the next, a small early lead grows until one option takes the whole market.**

1989 · W. Brian Arthur, with Paul A. David · [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.2307/2234208) · Feeds 1 axis

## The idea

Most economics assumed returns that shrink. The more of something you make or buy, the less each extra unit is worth, so markets settle on a balance that history doesn't change. The economist W. Brian Arthur argued that much of the modern economy works the other way. A keyboard layout, a video format or a piece of software is worth more to you the more other people use it.

In 1989 Arthur set out a model in *The Economic Journal*. Two technologies compete for adopters who arrive one at a time. Some adopters prefer one and some the other, but each new user of a technology makes it a little more attractive to the next. Early on, the order in which people happen to arrive tips the balance one way or the other. Sooner or later one technology pulls far enough ahead that every newcomer picks it. Arthur called this lock-in. Run the same model again with the arrivals in a different order and the other technology can win.

The trait this picks out is whether a lead feeds itself. Where it does, early chance events decide much of the outcome, and the result can't be read from the merits of the options alone.

## What goes wrong

People assume the market picked the winner because it was best. Under increasing returns the winner may just have got there first, so copying it or backing it says little about quality. The opposite mistake is to wait for the facts before you move. By the time it is clear which option will win, the lead has often locked in and the chance to shape it has gone.

## What to do instead

Move early when leads feed themselves, because a small push then is worth far more than a large one later. Before you back a leader, ask whether it leads on merit or on timing. Check whether a market has locked in before you try to overturn it, since a better rival may not break in on merit alone.

## Examples

### QWERTY

In 1985 the economic historian Paul A. David told the story of the QWERTY keyboard. It was set out for typewriters in the 1870s. Once typists learnt it, firms bought machines with the layout typists knew, and typists learnt the layout firms bought. David argued that this loop kept QWERTY in place long after other layouts claimed to be faster.

### The fable of the keys

In 1990 the economists Stan Liebowitz and Stephen Margolis looked again at the evidence that other layouts were faster. They found the key studies weak, and some were run by the inventor of a rival layout. They argued QWERTY was about as good as its rivals, so it had locked in without being worse. The case shows that lock-in is real but proving the market chose badly is hard.
