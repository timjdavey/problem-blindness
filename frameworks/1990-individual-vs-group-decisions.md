# Individual vs group decisions

In the late 1980s Donald Redelmeier was a young doctor training at Stanford, and Amos Tversky was a psychologist there. They asked whether doctors decide differently for one patient than for a group of patients just like them. In April 1990 they published the answer in the *New England Journal of Medicine*. Over 350 doctors at Stanford, a county hospital and a regional health maintenance organisation got the same cases. Half got a version about one patient and half got a version about a group. One case was a college student with fatigue, poor sleep and trouble concentrating. Should the doctor order an extra blood test that might find a rare but treatable condition, at a cost of $20 to the student? For the single patient, 30% of doctors ordered it. For the group, only 17% did.

Other cases pointed the same way. The authors asked a panel of doctors trained in health research to compare the two views directly. Of these, 93% said doctors would raise organ donation with a healthy young motorcyclist more often when thinking about a group. And 81% said the extra blood test would be ordered more often when thinking about one patient. The authors wrote that "the discrepancy between the aggregate and the individual perspectives exists in the mind of the physician". So this isn't a clash between what the patient wants and what society wants. The same doctor gives two answers to one question. The trait this sorts on is whether a decision is about one case or is a policy for many.

The mistake is treating a case as unique when it is really one of many. Each patient feels special, so the extra test seems worth it and the awkward talk about organ donation can wait. But a doctor who sees the same problem every week is setting a policy whether they mean to or not. One of the two answers must be wrong, because the same patient can't deserve both. The better response is to ask what you would want done for every patient like this one. Then check whether this patient really differs in a way that matters.

No single axis covers this framework. [Reading the axes](../axes/README.md) uses it to ask whether you are deciding one case or setting a policy for many.

## Examples

**Samuelson's lunch bet.** In a 1963 paper the economist Paul Samuelson told how he once offered some colleagues at lunch a bet on a coin toss. They would win $200 or lose $100. One of them turned it down. "I won't bet because I would feel the $100 loss more than the $200 gain," he said. "But I'll take you on if you promise to let me make 100 such bets." Samuelson argued that the two answers can't both be right. Under the standard theory, someone who turns down one such bet should turn down a hundred too. Either way, the colleague gave a different answer to the same bet depending on whether he saw it alone or as one of a series.

**Twenty-three executives.** In his 2015 book *Misbehaving*, Richard Thaler describes a meeting with the executives of a profitable print media firm. Each ran their own division. He asked whether they would take on a project with a 50% chance of making $2 million and a 50% chance of losing $1 million. Only 3 of the 23 said yes. Then he asked the chief executive how many such projects he would want, if they didn't depend on each other. He wanted all of them. Together the 23 projects would be expected to make $11.5 million, with under a 5% chance of losing money overall. Each manager saw one risky bet, while the boss saw a policy.
