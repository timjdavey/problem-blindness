# Constrained vs unconstrained skills

**Some skills are a small, closed set that nearly everyone masters in a few years. Others have no ceiling and keep growing for life.**

2005 · Scott G. Paris · [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.1598/RRQ.40.2.3) · Feeds 1 axis

## The idea

In 2005 the reading researcher Scott Paris pointed out that the skills children learn on the way to reading come in two kinds. Some cover a small, fixed body of knowledge. There are 26 letters, and almost every child learns them all within a few years. Others have no such limit. Nobody ever finishes learning words, and an adult's vocabulary keeps growing for decades. Paris called the first kind constrained and the second unconstrained. The trait this sorts on is whether a skill has a ceiling that everyone reaches.

## What goes wrong

The mistake is to measure both kinds the same way. Test letter knowledge at age four and the scores spread out nicely, so it seems to predict who will read well later. Test the same children at seven and nearly all of them score full marks, so the link vanishes. Paris argued that much of the research on early reading had read these short lived links as causes. It concluded that drilling the letters would make children better readers. But a constrained skill only stands in for something else while children are still learning it. Teach it hard and you get a quick gain that fades once the others catch up.

## What to do instead

The better response is to treat the two kinds differently. A constrained skill should be taught until it is mastered, then checked off. Its score says whether a child has it, not how good a reader they will be. An unconstrained skill needs years of steady growth, and it needs a measure that never runs out of room.

## Examples

### Letters against comprehension

Paris's own case. Knowing the alphabet is constrained. Understanding what you read is not, since there is always a harder text and a deeper reading. A school that judges its early reading programme by letter scores will see fast gains in the first year and learn little about whether its pupils will read well at eleven.

### Benchmarks that fill up

AI models show the same pattern. A test like MMLU, a bank of multiple choice questions across 57 subjects, spread models out well when it came out in 2020. By 2024 the best models scored around 90%, and the gaps between them were too small to trust. Labs moved to harder sets such as MMLU-Pro and Humanity's Last Exam. A full score on a test with a ceiling tells you the model has that slice. It doesn't tell you how far its skill goes beyond it.
