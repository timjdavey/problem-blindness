# Signal detection theory

**Any yes or no call can go wrong in two ways. You can raise a false alarm or you can miss the real thing.**

1954 · Wilson Tanner and John Swets · [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0058700) · Feeds 1 axis

## The idea

In the early 1950s engineers at the University of Michigan worked out how a radar set should decide whether a faint blip was a real target or just noise. Wilson Tanner and John Swets, two psychologists there, saw that a person straining to see a dim light faces the same problem. In 1954 they published "A decision-making theory of visual detection" in Psychological Review. In 1966 David Green and Swets turned the idea into a textbook called *Signal Detection Theory and Psychophysics*, and it spread from there into medicine and machine learning.

Every yes or no call has four outcomes. Saying yes to a real signal is a hit, and saying no to it is a miss. Saying yes to plain noise is a false alarm, and saying no to it is a correct rejection. Tanner and Swets split performance into two separate parts. One is how well you can tell signal from noise at all. The other is how much evidence you demand before you say yes, which is a choice. Lower the bar and you catch more real cases but raise more false alarms. Raise it and you get the reverse. No bar gets rid of both errors, so the best one depends on how common the signal is and what each error costs. The trait this sorts on is whether false alarms and misses cost the same.

Later work found the same logic built into living things. In 2000 Martie Haselton and David Buss proposed error management theory. Where two errors cost different amounts over the course of evolution, minds lean towards the cheaper one. They used it to explain why men tend to overrate a woman's sexual interest and women tend to underrate a man's commitment. In 2001 Randolph Nesse called it the smoke detector principle. Defences like a cough, anxiety or running away are cheap next to the harm they guard against, so the best system raises many false alarms. That is why your smoke alarm goes off for burnt toast, and why it should.

## What goes wrong

The mistake is to judge a test or a person by how often they are right. When one error costs far more than the other, the most accurate rule can be the wrong one. A smoke alarm that never sounds is right on almost every day of the year, and it is useless.

## What to do instead

The better response is to ask two questions in turn. First, how well can you tell the cases apart? Then, what does each kind of error cost? Set the bar from those costs and from how common the real thing is.

## Examples

### Blackstone's ratio

In 1769 William Blackstone wrote in his *Commentaries on the Laws of England* that "it is better that ten guilty persons escape, than that one innocent suffer". That is a bar set by cost. A wrongful conviction counts as ten times worse than a wrongful acquittal, so the proof needed for guilt goes up. John Fortescue had put the ratio at twenty to one around 1470. English law still uses two bars. Criminal courts need proof beyond reasonable doubt, while civil courts decide on the balance of probabilities, where a wrong verdict usually costs money rather than liberty.

### Breast screening

In 2003 Rebecca Smith-Bindman and her colleagues compared about 5.5 million mammograms from the United States and the United Kingdom. Among women aged 50 to 54 at their first screen, 12.5% to nearly 15% were called back for more tests in the two American programmes, against 7.6% in the British one. Yet both countries found about the same number of cancers, around six for every 1,000 mammograms. The lower American bar found no more cancers and gave many more women a scare. The authors pointed to fear of being sued for a missed cancer as one cause. In 2011 a study led by Rebecca Hubbard found that 61% of American women screened every year from age 40 get at least one false alarm within ten years.
