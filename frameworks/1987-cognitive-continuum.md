# Cognitive continuum

**Tasks sit on a line from intuition to analysis. People do best when the way they think matches where the task sits.**

1987 · Kenneth R. Hammond, Robert Hamm, Janet Grassia and Tamra Pearson · [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.1109/TSMC.1987.6499282) · Feeds 1 axis

## The idea

Most talk about thinking splits it in two: gut feel or careful analysis. Kenneth Hammond, an American psychologist who studied judgement, argued it is a line, not a switch. At one end is pure intuition, which is quick and hard to explain. At the other is pure analysis, which is slow and step by step, so you can show every move. Most real thinking sits somewhere between, in a blend he called quasi-rationality.

The twist is that tasks sit on a line too. A task with many cues shown at once and no clear rule for combining them pulls towards intuition. A task with few cues, a known formula and time to work pulls towards analysis. Hammond's claim is that people do best when the way they think matches where the task sits. So the trait this sorts on is the shape of the task: how many cues there are, how clear the rules are and how much time you have.

## What goes wrong

Getting the match wrong fails both ways. Force analysis onto a task full of cues and you pick the few you can measure, ignore the rest and do worse than a gut call. Use gut feel on a task with a clear rule and you add noise to something a formula would get right every time.

## What to do instead

The better response is to read the task before you choose how to think. Count the cues, check for a known rule and see how much time you have.

## Examples

### The highway engineers

In their 1987 study, Hammond and his colleagues asked experienced highway engineers to judge stretches of road on three tasks: how good each road looked, how safe it was and how much traffic it could carry. Each task was shown in three ways. Film of the road invited intuition, bar graphs of the measures sat in the middle and a formula invited analysis. Analysis did not win the way you might expect. Intuition often did as well or better, and the engineers tended to do best when the way they thought matched the task.

### Wine and tax

Choosing a wine you like suits intuition. There are many cues at once and no formula to combine them. Doing your tax return suits analysis, because there are clear rules and one right answer. Nobody tries to sniff a tax form. The harder cases sit in the middle, like a doctor reading a chest X-ray. Trained pattern spotting does most of the work, but a checklist catches what the eye skipped.
