# Agency costs

Picture a woman who owns her firm outright. If she buys a lavish office with company money, every pound of it is her own. Now say she sells 90% of the firm to outside shareholders. The same office now costs her ten pence in the pound, so she is far more tempted to buy it. Michael Jensen and William Meckling, two economists at the University of Rochester, built a theory of the firm on this point. They published it in October 1976 in the Journal of Financial Economics as "Theory of the firm: managerial behavior, agency costs and ownership structure". It became one of the most cited papers in finance.

The person who decides is called the agent. The person who bears the result is called the principal. Owners can spend money watching the manager, and the manager can spend money to prove they can be trusted. Some value is still lost after both. Jensen and Meckling called the sum of all three agency costs. In 2018 Nassim Nicholas Taleb restated the idea for a wide audience in his book *Skin in the Game*. His rule is that whoever gains when things go well should also lose when they go wrong.

The trait this sorts on is whether whoever decides bears the cost of being wrong. This is not the same as [accountability](1999-accountability.md), which asks whether you must explain a choice. Here the question is whether you pay for it. A banker can write a careful memo for every loan and still keep his bonus when the loans go bad. The mistake is to treat a decision or a forecast as if the person making it shared your stakes. The better response is to ask who pays if it goes wrong. If it isn't the person deciding, give them a stake, check their work harder or keep the decision yourself. An AI agent is the extreme case. It never bears the cost of being wrong, because it loses no money, no job and no sleep. All the checking has to come from the people who will pay.

This framework feeds the [Skin in the game vs none](../axes/skin-in-the-game-vs-none.md) axis.

## Examples

**Hammurabi's builder.** In about 1753 BC the Babylonian king Hammurabi had his laws carved on a basalt stele 2.25 metres tall. It was found at Susa in present day Iran in 1901 and now stands in the Louvre. Law 229 says that if a builder builds a house badly and it falls and kills the owner, the builder shall be put to death. Law 232 says that if it ruins the owner's goods, the builder pays for them and rebuilds the house at his own cost. The builder knows where the shortcuts are hidden and the owner can't see them, so the law made the builder carry the risk. Taleb quotes this law early in *Skin in the Game*.

**Subprime mortgages.** Before 2008 many American lenders sold the loans they made to banks, which bundled them into bonds. If a borrower stopped paying, the bond buyer lost, not the lender who had checked the borrower. Benjamin Keys, Tanmoy Mukherjee, Amit Seru and Vikrant Vig studied more than one million subprime home loans made from 2001 to 2006. Loan buyers used a credit score of 620 as a rule of thumb, so loans just above it were much easier to sell. Among loans with little paperwork, more than twice as many were sold just above 620 as just below. Yet those loans defaulted within two years 10 to 25% more often, even though the borrowers on either side of the line were almost the same. Lenders checked less carefully when they knew someone else would carry the loss. The study was published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2010.
