# Clinical vs statistical prediction

There are two ways to make a forecast about a person. An expert can read the file, meet them and weigh it all up in their head. Or you can write down a few facts, plug them into a fixed formula and take whatever number comes out. The psychologist Paul Meehl called the first clinical prediction and the second statistical prediction. In his 1954 book he gathered about twenty studies that tested both on the same cases, such as who would do well at college. The formulas matched or beat the experts in nearly all of them.

Later reviews of well over a hundred studies found the same thing. The tasks where formulas win share a shape. The same kind of decision comes up again and again, the outcome is noisy and the expert rarely gets clear feedback on how each call turned out. In that setting a person's judgement drifts. They give weight to vivid details, they get tired and they are swayed by mood. A formula gives the same answer to the same facts every time, and that alone beats most experts.

The trait this sorts on is whether a problem is a repeated, noisy prediction or a rare case with facts no formula could hold. The usual mistake is to treat the first kind as the second. Experts feel each case is special and trust their gut, so they throw away the gains a simple rule would give. Meehl also warned about the reverse. He imagined a formula that says a professor goes to the cinema every Tuesday. If you know he broke his leg this morning, you should overrule it. The skill is knowing which kind of problem you have.

## Examples

**The Apgar score.** In 1952 the anaesthetist Virginia Apgar proposed a simple check on newborn babies. A doctor or midwife scores five things, such as heart rate and breathing, from 0 to 2 one minute after birth. Before that, doctors judged a baby's health by eye and often disagreed. The score made the call the same every time, and it is still used around the world. It checks the baby's state now rather than predicting the future, but it shows the same gain from a fixed rule over judgement by eye.

**Bordeaux wine.** In 1990 the economist Orley Ashenfelter published a formula that predicted the price of Bordeaux vintages from the weather. It used winter rain, summer heat and rain at harvest. Wine critics mocked the idea that a sum could judge wine. Yet the formula could rate a vintage as soon as the harvest was in, long before the wine was ready to drink, and it tracked later prices well.

**Job interviews.** Most firms hire by unstructured chat, where the interviewer forms an overall feel for the candidate. Studies of hiring keep finding that structured interviews do better. In those, every candidate gets the same questions and is scored on the same scale. A firm that sticks to the chat is treating a noisy, repeated prediction as if it were a one-off judgement.
