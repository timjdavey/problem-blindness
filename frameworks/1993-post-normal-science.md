# Post-normal science

Most science works on puzzles where the facts can be pinned down and little is at stake while you work. You run the test, check the result and publish. In 1993 the philosophers of science Silvio Funtowicz and Jerome Ravetz pointed to a different kind of question in the journal *Futures*. The facts are uncertain, people's values clash, the stakes are high and a decision is needed now. Waiting for the evidence to settle is itself a decision, and often a costly one. They called science done in these conditions post-normal.

They sorted problems on two traits: how uncertain the system is and how much rides on the decision. When both are low, ordinary applied science works. When both are moderate, you need the judgement of a skilled professional, like a surgeon or an engineer. When either is high, no expert can settle the question alone. The quality of the answer has to be judged by a wider group that includes the people affected. They called this an extended peer community.

The error is to treat a post-normal question as a normal one. Experts are asked for the answer, give a confident one and present value choices as if they were facts. When the answer turns out wrong, or the public sees the values hidden inside it, trust in science itself takes the blow. The better response is to say plainly where the facts end and the value choices begin, and to bring the people affected into judging the answer.

## Examples

**Mad cow disease.** In the late 1980s British cattle began dying of BSE, and nobody knew whether it could pass to people. Ministers leaned on scientists for reassurance. In May 1990 the agriculture minister John Gummer was filmed feeding his young daughter a burger. In March 1996 the government announced that BSE was the likely cause of a new human disease, variant CJD. The facts had been uncertain all along, but a judgement about acceptable risk had been sold to the public as settled science.

**Covid lockdowns.** In March 2020 governments had to decide on lockdowns with little data on how the virus spread or how deadly it was. The stakes were lives, jobs and schooling, and the deadline was days away. The slogan "follow the science" made it sound as if experts held the answer. In truth the choice weighed one set of harms against another, and that is a question of values science can't answer alone.
