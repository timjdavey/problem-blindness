# Goodhart's law

**When a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.**

1975 · Charles Goodhart · [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-349-17295-5_4) · Feeds 2 axes

## The idea

A thermometer doesn't care what you think of the temperature. You can read it as often as you like and the weather stays the same. A number about people is different. Once people know you are watching it and will reward or punish them by it, they start working on the number instead of the thing it was meant to show. The link that made the number useful then breaks.

Charles Goodhart, an economist at the Bank of England, put it this way in 1975. Any pattern you spot in the data will tend to collapse once you lean on it for control. He was thinking of money supply targets in Britain. In 1997 the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern gave the idea its famous short form: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

## What goes wrong

The framework sorts problems by whether the thing you measure can react to being measured. Where it can't, more measurement gives a sharper picture. Where it can, every new target changes the problem. The mistake is to treat the second kind like the first. You pick a number and tie rewards to it, then trust the reports as if they were thermometer readings. What you get back is a measure of how well people can hit the number. [Campbell's law](1976-campbells-law.md) adds that the work itself gets bent, and the [Lucas critique](1976-lucas-critique.md) makes the same point about economic models.

## What to do instead

The better response is to keep the number you reward apart from the number you learn from, and to change targets before people learn to game them.

## Examples

### Hanoi's rats

In 1902 the French rulers of Hanoi were fighting a plague of rats in the city's new sewers. They paid a bounty for each rat tail handed in. Soon tailless rats were seen running around the city. People had cut off the tails and let the rats go to breed, and some began farming rats for the reward. The count of tails went up while the number of rats did not fall.

### Wells Fargo

For years the American bank Wells Fargo pushed staff to sell each customer more products, under the slogan "eight is great". In September 2016 it was fined $185 million after staff were found to have opened around two million accounts that customers never asked for. The number of accounts per customer looked superb. It had stopped telling managers anything about how happy or loyal those customers were.

### Monetary targets

Goodhart's own case came soon after his warning. In 1980 the British government set targets for the growth of the money supply, on the grounds that it had tracked inflation in the past. Once it became a target, the old link fell apart. The money supply overshot its targets in most years even as inflation fell, and by 1986 the target for broad money had been dropped.
