# Axes for sorting problems

Each axis is a scale with two ends, and any problem sits somewhere along it. They fall into three groups. The first is about the world you act on. The second is about what you can know about it. The third is about the decision itself, what it needs and what it costs to get wrong.

The last column, Correlated with, lists the other axes each one tends to move with. On every axis the first end is the easy one and the second is the hard one. So "correlated" means a problem near the hard end of one axis tends to sit near the hard end of the other. A few entries say "opposite" when the hard end of one goes with the easy end of the other. "Tension" marks two hard ends that clash when they meet, and "independent" marks two axes that look linked but aren't.

Each entry also says where the link comes from. "By definition" means one axis is partly built into the other, so tagging both may count the same thing twice. "Bundled" means a framework puts both under one label. "Study" means there is evidence or a worked case showing them together. "Logic" means it follows from what the two axes mean.

Needing the best answer when the land is rugged or the search is too big is the hardest case. Small steps stop on the nearest peak, and the [NP problems](frameworks/1971-np-problems.md) note advises settling for a good answer. **Single vs multiple objectives** (opposite). "Best" needs one agreed score, so several goals push you towards good enough. **Easy vs hard to verify** (tension). You can't claim you found the best answer if you can't check it. |
| Easy vs hard to verify | Can you check an answer cheaply, even if finding it was hard? A solved sudoku takes seconds to check. A business strategy or a diagnosis may take years to prove right or wrong. | [Well vs ill-structured problems](frameworks/1964-well-vs-ill-structured-problems.md), [NP problems](frameworks/1971-np-problems.md), [Tame vs wicked problems](frameworks/1973-tame-vs-wicked-problems.md) | **Inert vs reflexive** (logic). By [Goodhart's law](frameworks/1975-goodharts-law.md) a cheap check stops working once people are judged on it. **Kind vs wicked feedback** (logic). Feedback is the world checking your answer for you, so a hard check means slow feedback. **Well-posed vs ill-posed** (by definition). A test for the answer is part of what Simon means by [well-structured](frameworks/1964-well-vs-ill-structured-problems.md). **Single vs multiple objectives** (logic). With no single score there is no simple check. **Satisficing vs optimising** (tension). You can't claim the best answer if you can't check it. **Tractable vs intractable search** (independent). [NP problems](frameworks/1971-np-problems.md) are the textbook case of hard to find and easy to check. |
| Tractable vs intractable search | How many options do you have to search through? Chess is fully visible, fixed and rule bound, yet the number of possible games is so vast that you can't check them all. | [NP problems](frameworks/1971-np-problems.md) | **Smooth vs rugged** (study). Stepping downhill on rugged land stops at a false bottom ([Convex vs non-convex optimisation](frameworks/1970-convex-vs-non-convex-optimisation.md)). Edward Weinberger proved in 1996 that finding the top of a general NK landscape is NP-complete. **Decomposable vs holistic** (logic). A problem that splits into parts can be searched one part at a time, like Simon's [watchmakers](frameworks/1962-near-decomposability.md). **Satisficing vs optimising** (tension). Needing the best answer from a search too big to finish is the hardest case. **Easy vs hard to verify** (independent). [NP problems](frameworks/1971-np-problems.md) are hard to find and easy to check. **Full vs partial observability** (independent). Chess hides nothing and still can't be searched in full. |
| One-shot vs sequential | Is it a single choice, or a chain where each step shapes the next? Choosing a paint colour is one shot. Running a project or treating a long illness is a series of moves, and it's hard to tell which early move caused the final result. | [Multi-armed bandit](frameworks/1933-multi-armed-bandit.md), [Exploration vs exploitation](frameworks/1991-exploration-vs-exploitation.md), [Task environment properties](frameworks/1995-task-environment-properties.md) | **Transparent vs opaque causes** and **Kind vs wicked feedback** (study). In Senge's [beer game](frameworks/1990-detail-vs-dynamic-complexity.md) delays between moves and results hide which move caused what. **Known vs unknown odds** (opposite). In a [bandit](frameworks/1933-multi-armed-bandit.md) problem each choice teaches you something, so repeating the choice slowly turns unknown odds into known ones. Watch the name clash. [Rittel and Webber](frameworks/1973-tame-vs-wicked-problems.md) call each try at a wicked problem a one-shot operation, but they mean it can't be undone. That belongs on the reversible vs irreversible axis, not this one. |
| Reversible vs irreversible | Can you undo it if it goes wrong? Trying a new recipe is a two way door. Selling the family home or cutting off a leg is a one way door. | [Tame vs wicked problems](frameworks/1973-tame-vs-wicked-problems.md), [One-way vs two-way doors](frameworks/2015-one-way-vs-two-way-doors.md) | **Survivable vs ruinous** (by definition). All ruin is irreversible, but not every irreversible choice ruins you. **Loose vs tight coupling** (study). In [Normal accidents](frameworks/1984-normal-accidents.md) faults cascade into damage that can't be undone before anyone can step in. **Kind vs wicked feedback** (bundled). [Rittel and Webber](frameworks/1973-tame-vs-wicked-problems.md) note you can't learn by trial and error when each try leaves a mark. **Familiar vs novel to the solver** (bundled). Simon's [non-programmed decisions](frameworks/1960-programmed-vs-non-programmed-decisions.md) are new and carry high stakes. **Free vs accountable** (logic). Bezos argues one-way doors deserve a slow process with many views ([One-way vs two-way doors](frameworks/2015-one-way-vs-two-way-doors.md)), and that brings scrutiny. |
| Survivable vs ruinous | Could one bad outcome take you out of the game for good? If so, the average result over many tries is useless, because you won't get many tries. Russian roulette has good odds and still isn't worth playing. | [Fourth quadrant](frameworks/2008-fourth-quadrant.md), [Fragile, robust and antifragile](frameworks/2012-fragile-robust-and-antifragile.md), [Ergodicity](frameworks/2016-ergodicity.md) | **Thin vs fat tails** (bundled). The [Fourth quadrant](frameworks/2008-fourth-quadrant.md) and [Ergodicity](frameworks/2016-ergodicity.md) both warn that one extreme loss can end the game. **Reversible vs irreversible** (by definition). Ruin is the worst kind of choice you can't undo. **Loose vs tight coupling** (study). Tight systems let small faults cascade into a meltdown ([Normal accidents](frameworks/1984-normal-accidents.md)). **Plentiful vs scarce data** (logic). You can't learn from your own ruin, because it only happens once. |
| Slack vs time pressure | How long do you have to decide, compared to how fast things change? A pension plan gives you months. A pilot with a failed engine has seconds. | [Post-normal science](frameworks/1993-post-normal-science.md), [Cynefin](frameworks/1999-cynefin.md), [Tame, wicked and critical](frameworks/2005-tame-wicked-and-critical.md), [Super wicked problems](frameworks/2007-super-wicked-problems.md) | **Loose vs tight coupling** (by definition). Perrow defines tight coupling as having no slack, so when a tight system fails it hands you a decision with no time ([Normal accidents](frameworks/1984-normal-accidents.md)). **Explicit vs tacit know-how** (study). Hammond's [cognitive continuum](frameworks/1987-cognitive-continuum.md) puts tasks with little time at the intuitive end, and Klein's fire lieutenant got out on a gut call ([Conditions for intuitive expertise](frameworks/2009-conditions-for-intuitive-expertise.md)). **Shared vs contested values** (bundled). [Post-normal science](frameworks/1993-post-normal-science.md) and [super wicked problems](frameworks/2007-super-wicked-problems.md) both join a clock to a fight over values. Grint warns that leaders sometimes call a problem a crisis to justify giving orders ([Tame, wicked and critical](frameworks/2005-tame-wicked-and-critical.md)), so check that the deadline is real. |
| Free vs accountable | Must you explain and defend the choice? A hunch is fine for picking a film. A loan refusal, a court ruling or a medical decision may have to be explained, audited or defended in law, which rules out some methods even when they would work better. | Novel | **Explicit vs tacit know-how** (tension). Know-how you can't put into words is hard to defend in an audit, so accountability pushes towards written rules like Meehl's [formulas](frameworks/1954-clinical-vs-statistical-prediction.md). **Inert vs reflexive** (study). ## Grain and horizon

Where a problem sits on most axes depends on how closely you look and over what time. One boiler breaking down is random, but a fleet of ten thousand breaks down at a steady rate. Weather is chaotic two weeks out, while climate is steady over decades. So before you place a problem on any axis, say what scale and what timescale you mean, or the same problem will get tagged two different ways.
